## Starting point and result

PyTorch, TMB and PyMC can all express custom probabilistic calculations, but they are often used to answer different questions. Maximum likelihood returns an optimizing parameter value. Laplace methods approximate integration over selected latent variables. Bayesian inference targets a posterior distribution given a likelihood and priors. Their outputs are not interchangeable merely because each uses derivatives.

## Mechanism: distinguish the target measure

For observed data y, MLE maximizes \(p(y\mid\theta)\). A marginal likelihood integrates latent variables u: \(p(y\mid\theta)=\int p(y,u\mid\theta)du\). Bayesian inference targets \(p(\theta,u\mid y)\propto p(y\mid\theta,u)p(u\mid\theta)p(\theta)\). MAP maximizes this posterior density in a declared parameterization; it is not the same object as a posterior mean or an MLE.

Automatic differentiation provides local derivatives of a declared computation. It does not decide whether that computation is a likelihood, posterior or approximation. Likewise, distributing several MCMC chains is different from distributing one likelihood evaluation across data partitions. Spark can prepare data or orchestrate independent chains, but that does not make a joint posterior factorize by arbitrary storage partitions.

A change of variables needs care. An optimization reparameterization may simply evaluate the same objective at transformed coordinates. A transformed probability density needs the appropriate Jacobian when defining the density in new variables. Confusing these purposes changes the target.

## Worked case: normal mean with a conjugate prior

Suppose observations have known variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(\theta\sim N(m_0,s_0^2)\). The posterior precision is \(n/\sigma^2+1/s_0^2\), and its mean is

\[m=\frac{\sum_i y_i/\sigma^2+m_0/s_0^2}{n/\sigma^2+1/s_0^2}.\]

The MLE is the sample mean. With finite prior precision, the posterior mean generally differs. This analytic case can validate a PyMC posterior or a MAP objective independently. Agreement should be judged against the correct target, not by demanding every library reproduce the MLE.

## Worked case: a non-Gaussian latent model

For a count model with random effects, a TMB Laplace approximation can make marginal optimization efficient. A PyMC implementation can explore the joint posterior with a suitable sampler, at a potentially different computational cost. Compare predictive quantities, uncertainty and approximation sensitivity on a small reference problem. A similar point estimate does not establish equivalent posterior tails or calibration.

For large data, consider whether sufficient statistics, minibatching with an appropriate inference method, or a valid additive likelihood reduces cost. Ordinary exact-gradient MCMC does not become correct by replacing the full gradient with an arbitrary minibatch gradient without algorithmic changes.

## Failure diagnosis

A difference between MLE and posterior summaries may be expected prior influence rather than a bug. Divergences or poor chain mixing are sampling diagnostics, while an optimizer's gradient norm is a stationarity diagnostic. Neither replaces posterior predictive checks or model criticism. Report them with their correct interpretation.

## Interview discussion

**Why retain Spark as a focus if PyMC is useful?** Spark addresses data preparation and distributed evaluation/orchestration. PyMC addresses probabilistic modeling and inference. They can complement each other when the boundary is explicit.

**Does a Hessian-based standard error equal a credible interval?** Only under additional approximations and assumptions. Local curvature, posterior mass and frequentist coverage are different concepts.

## Separate an estimate, a declared point and a posterior draw

The native package's [named-target API](../../../../packages/spark-tmbad/python/docs/EXTENDED-TARGETS.md)
records Gaussian means/precisions, selected L1 penalties and fixed coordinates.
An optimizing result carries fit status and its target definition. A
`PreparedParameters` object instead declares a source-bound parameter point for
simulation truth, sensitivity analysis or an externally obtained posterior draw.
It can predict and forecast without manufacturing a convergence trace. Naming
a point "posterior draw" does not execute a sampler or verify its provenance.

For the conjugate Gaussian case, the
[optional R reference](../../../../packages/spark-tmbad/reference/docs/INFERENCE.md)
computes a proper Gaussian posterior and draws independent samples from it.
Its posterior draw-mean Monte Carlo error measures numerical sampling error
conditional on one fixed observed dataset. Repeating those draws is different
from repeatedly generating new datasets, fitting each one, and measuring an
estimator's bias or predictive coverage. The reference aggregator explicitly
withholds independent-dataset Monte Carlo error when the training data repeats.

A prior applies to named optimizer coordinates. A Gaussian prior on
`log_sigma` describes uncertainty on a log scale. A prior specified on `sigma`
has a different density when expressed on that coordinate and may need a
Jacobian. Fixed coordinates are declared constraints, not observations with
infinite precision. The native implementation evaluates an all-fixed vector
once and records that status rather than reporting fictitious optimization.

The distinction also matters for forecasts. The correlated Gaussian random-slope
model integrates its latent intercept/slope exactly and supplies an
`existing_group` target conditional on explicit history. Its marginal per-row
variance includes latent and observation uncertainty at chosen population
parameters. It does not include uncertainty in those population parameters.
Future rows within a group remain correlated even when the public output returns
only marginal means and variances.

The nonlinear reference makes the approximation visible. Its new-group mean
integrates a lognormal latent amplitude; plugging in a zero latent effect would
give a different answer. A local Laplace calculation is compared with independent
quadrature and retains the approximation difference. Mean-plus-normal-quantile
times mixture standard deviation is labeled a moment-based interval, not an
exact mixture quantile. Read the
[qualified case and simulation records](../../../../packages/spark-tmbad/reference/QUALIFICATION.md)
before choosing which uncertainty statement supports a scientific conclusion.
