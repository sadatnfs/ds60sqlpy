## From relative scores to a choice distribution

Suppose a model gives two next-token candidates scores 0 and log(3). A score is not yet a probability: scores may be negative, may exceed one and need not sum to one. What matters initially is their relative preference. Softmax turns those scores into positive weights, then divides each weight by their total. The exponential of 0 is 1 and the exponential of log(3) is 3, so the normalized probabilities are 1/4 and 3/4. The second candidate receives three times as much probability as the first.

Let z_i denote the score of candidate i, with i ranging over the V allowed vocabulary entries. Write p_i for its resulting probability and Z for the sum of the exponential weights. Then Z=sum_j exp(z_j) and p_i=exp(z_i)/Z. The index j in the sum runs over candidate choices; it does not run over different questions or different token positions. For a batch of questions, each question needs its own denominator. This is why an axis argument is part of the meaning of the calculation, not a minor array detail.

The ratio p_i/p_j equals exp(z_i-z_j). The shared denominator cancels, so subtracting the same number from every score leaves all probability ratios unchanged. That observation gives the stable implementation. For [1000,1000], computing exp(1000) directly is unnecessary and may overflow. Subtract the maximum, producing [0,0], exponentiate to [1,1], then normalize to [0.5,0.5]. The equal probabilities are not an approximation introduced by the shift. They are exactly the distribution that the original equal scores describe, subject to ordinary floating-point rounding.

A mask changes the candidate set over which the denominator is defined. In the fixture [0,100,0] with only the first and third positions allowed, the middle score must contribute neither mass nor a stabilizing maximum. The valid calculation is therefore the two-way competition between equal scores, resulting in [0.5,0,0.5]. If the huge forbidden score is included first, it takes almost all the normalized mass. Deleting its probability afterward leaves a row that scarcely sums to anything. The error is a change in the probability experiment, not simply a wrong final display value.

Temperature changes score differences rather than shifting them. Dividing [0,log(3)] by two reduces the odds ratio from 3 to sqrt(3), spreading probability more evenly while preserving the ranking. A common offset preserves the distribution; a positive scale generally changes it. This difference will matter when we choose how to sample text. It also explains why two systems with the same most likely token can behave differently: one may put 0.51 on the winner, another 0.99.

Softmax is useful when exactly one candidate is to be selected from a defined set, as in next-token prediction or one attention row. It is not a reason to force mutually independent yes/no events into a single competition. Nor does normalization calibrate a model against reality. It guarantees a categorical distribution under its input scores and allowed set; factual or task reliability remains an empirical question.
