# dme-012: Facts, dimensions and slowly changing business meaning

## Model the question before normalizing the tables

A delivery fact records an identified business occurrence and its measurements. A route dimension describes the route associated with that occurrence. Separating these concerns can make a data product easier to query, but only when the grain and relationship are explicit. A familiar table name does not establish which version of a description a fact should use.

The example uses actual DuckDB tables. `deliveries` has one row per delivery, including route identity, package count and a business event date. `history` contains versioned route descriptions with valid-from and valid-to dates. A composite primary key protects the identity of each route/version boundary, and a check constraint requires an end date to follow its start date when an end is present.

The dates use one declared reporting calendar. They are calendar dates rather than precise aware instants. A system with changes within a day would need a more precise timestamp and timezone contract. Neither a date type nor a historical table name tells you when a description became available to an earlier decision-maker; that additional clock is developed in the temporal-feature chapters.

## Distinguish fact grain from dimension grain

A fact table's grain states what one row represents: a delivery, an order line, a measurement or a periodic snapshot, for example. Measures should be interpreted at that grain. Combining delivery-level counts with route-level totals in the same apparent relation can create double counting or misleading averages.

A dimension supplies descriptive attributes under an identity and version policy. Some attributes are stable, while others change. A route can move from one region to another without becoming a different route. A current-state dimension records the latest description. A versioned dimension can retain earlier business-valid descriptions.

A surrogate dimension key is one common way to identify a particular versioned row independently of its business key. A fact can then reference the intended version explicitly. The example instead demonstrates a natural route key plus an interval join so that the temporal rule is visible in the SQL. Both approaches need a reviewed policy for late corrections and consumer interpretation.

Do not assume every repeated dimension business key is an accidental duplicate. It may represent legitimate historical versions. Conversely, adding valid-from and valid-to columns does not automatically make those versions non-overlapping, complete or correct. The relationship must still be validated.

## Use half-open validity intervals deliberately

Each history row is valid from its start date, inclusive, until its end date, exclusive. A missing end means that the interval remains open in this declared business history. For a fact date \(t\), the interval condition is

\[
a\le t<b,
\]

where \(a\) is the start and \(b\) is the end when one exists. This half-open convention lets adjacent versions meet at a boundary without both matching that boundary date.

Route 10 belongs to `north` from 1 January up to 1 February and to `west` from 1 February onward. A delivery on 1 February therefore uses the new `west` interval. Using an inclusive end on the first interval as well would create a double match at the transition unless another rule resolved it.

The convention must be consistent across producers, transformations and consumers. An end date that means “last valid day” is a different representation from an exclusive boundary. Converting between them requires care, especially when moving from dates to timestamps or across calendars.

## Worked case 1: Two legitimate questions produce different classifications

The three facts are delivery 1 on 15 January for route 10 with two packages, delivery 2 on 1 February for route 10 with three packages, and delivery 3 on 2 February for route 20 with one package. Route 20 remains in `south` throughout the fixture.

The historical interval join assigns delivery 1 to `north`, delivery 2 to `west` and delivery 3 to `south`. The program checks that every fact has exactly one matching history row. The boundary-day observation specifically confirms the exclusive-end/inclusive-start convention.

A second actual query joins all deliveries to the current open-ended descriptions. It assigns both route-10 deliveries to `west`. That can answer a different legitimate question, such as attributing historical activity to current route ownership. It is not the same as reporting the region that was business-valid on each event date.

The grand package total remains six under both classifications. A total-only test therefore cannot distinguish the two interpretations. The identified rows provide the stronger oracle. Historical classification yields north two, west three and south one; current classification yields west five and south one. Choose the query according to the consumer's question and label its meaning explicitly.

This business-valid history does not by itself prove point-in-time suitability for model training. A corrected history row may have been loaded after the prediction cutoff. A training feature needs the relevant availability or system-time evidence as well as a valid-time relationship.

## Constraints do not cover every temporal relationship

The composite primary key ensures that one route/start-date combination is not repeated. It does not prohibit another version with a different start date from overlapping an existing interval. The end-after-start check rejects a malformed individual interval, but it does not compare every interval with its neighbors.

A useful validation therefore inspects both interval relationships and fact matches. Counting matches for each fact detects missing and duplicated classifications in the observed population. An independent interval-overlap query can detect a problem even when no current fact happens to fall inside the overlapping period.

This distinction is important for testing. A dataset can appear healthy under today's facts while containing history that will duplicate a later backfill. Validate the dimension's own contract as well as its interaction with the current fact table.

## Worked case 2: Overlapping history and an unknown route block acceptance

The changed case inserts an `east` description for route 10 spanning 10 January through 10 February. Its start date is distinct, so it satisfies the composite primary key. Its end follows its start, so it also satisfies the row-level interval check. It nevertheless overlaps both the earlier `north` interval and the later `west` interval.

The case also inserts a fact for unknown route 99. The left-join cardinality check reports two matches for deliveries 1 and 2, one for delivery 3 and zero for delivery 4. The independent overlap query reports two overlapping interval pairs for route 10.

The program declares the candidate unpublishable. It does not select an arbitrary matching region, divide an inflated aggregate by a guessed factor or discard the unknown route without accounting for it. A correction needs a reviewed source or business rule. The diagnostics identify which relationship failed so the repair can be investigated.

All of these queries run against real local tables. The example does not establish the enforcement behavior of an unrelated warehouse or lake format. If a chosen production engine treats some constraints as informational, use actual validation gates and inspect their outcomes before publishing the product.

## Diagnose historical-modeling errors

First identify the intended question: current classification, business-valid historical classification or information available at a past decision. These can require different tables and predicates. An apparently wrong result may reflect a mismatched question rather than a parser failure.

Next inspect the key and interval contracts. Are start boundaries unique where required? Are intervals well formed and non-overlapping? Are gaps permitted? Does every fact require a match, or is an explicit unknown member part of the design? Check the actual identities and multiplicities rather than relying on a grand total that may remain unchanged.

Finally, inspect correction and backfill behavior. A new dimension version can change the interpretation of existing facts. Decide whether prior reports are restated, retained under an earlier release or exposed through versioned views. A silent change in historical meaning can be as disruptive as a broken query.

## Where this is useful

Use these models for organizational changes, product categories, account attributes, device ownership, route assignments and analytical reporting. Feature engineering adds another requirement: the data used for a historical prediction must have been known under the intended information boundary. The later feature-store material uses independent temporal oracles to test that requirement.

## Answered interview discussion

**Why use half-open intervals?** Adjacent versions can meet at one boundary without both claiming it. The rule still needs to match the producer's representation and the consumer's intended calendar or time precision.

**Does a composite primary key prevent overlapping history?** No. Different start dates can still define overlapping intervals. Validate the interval relationship separately.

**Why check both overlaps and fact cardinality?** Fact cardinality diagnoses the currently observed join result. An overlap check can expose invalid dimension history even when no current fact exercises the problematic period.

**When is a current-state join appropriate?** When the question deliberately uses current attributes. It should not be silently substituted for historical classification or for information available at a past prediction cutoff.

**How should late corrections be handled?** Define the accepted source, affected interval, consumer compatibility and restatement policy. Retain versioned evidence so users can explain which history produced a particular result.
