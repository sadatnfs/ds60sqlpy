## Information is not the same as optimizer convergence

A parameter is identifiable when distinct parameter values imply distinct observable distributions within the model. Weak identification means available observations distinguish some values only slightly. An optimizer can stop with finite coefficients in either situation. Its return code describes its algorithm, not the uniqueness or scientific precision of the estimate.

For a quadratic Gaussian mean model with known variance one,

\[
\mathcal L(\beta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\beta\|^2,\qquad H=X^\top X.
\]

A nonzero vector \(v\) satisfying \(Xv=0\) leaves fitted means unchanged when \(\beta\) is replaced by \(\beta+cv\). The Hessian has zero curvature in that direction. Adding a small numerical diagonal to make its inverse exist does not create data information about that direction.

## Worked case 1: duplicate design columns

The first fixture uses two identical predictor columns. Coefficients \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) have the same sum and therefore the same predictions and NLL. The Hessian eigenvalues are zero and 60. The zero eigenvalue precisely identifies a direction that trades one coefficient against the other without changing the observations.

The appropriate response depends on the scientific question. Remove a redundant column, reparameterize in terms of the identified sum, impose a justified constraint, or state a prior that supplies additional information. Reporting a pseudoinverse covariance as if both original coefficients were separately estimated with ordinary uncertainty would hide the identification issue.

This example is exact nonidentification. Near-duplicate columns produce small positive eigenvalues instead of exact zero, which can make fitted coefficients highly sensitive to small data changes. Numerical rank thresholds depend on scale and precision, so singular-value diagnostics should accompany domain reasoning rather than replacing it.

## Worked case 2: profile a nonlinear decay rate

For the curve \(m(t)=a e^{-bt}+c\), hold a candidate rate \(b\) fixed and refit \(a\) and \(c\) by least squares. This produces the profile objective

\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{prof}}(b)=\min_{a,c}\mathcal L(a,b,c).
\]

Profiling differs from evaluating a slice while keeping \(a\) and \(c\) fixed. A slice can appear sharply curved simply because nuisance parameters were not allowed to compensate. The program creates exact observations from \((a,b,c)=(2,0.7,0.4)\), uses a known scale 0.1, and evaluates a rate grid from 0.3 to 1.1.

For times spanning zero to six, the profile clearly prefers the generating rate. For times spanning zero to 0.08, the objective changes by only about \(2.46\times10^{-5}\) across the entire grid, compared with a long-window contrast near 24.85. The short-window approximation \(m(t)\approx(a+c)-ab t\) explains the ambiguity: the combinations of parameters are much easier to identify than each parameter individually.

The reference amplitude and offset are solved as unrestricted linear coefficients at each positive rate. That is the declared profile model here. If a substantive model requires nonnegative amplitude or baseline, the profiling step must solve the constrained nuisance problem instead. A profile over the wrong nuisance domain is not the same likelihood analysis.

## Uncertainty and diagnostic limits

A profile likelihood can reveal asymmetric or nearly flat information that a local quadratic approximation obscures. Turning profile differences into confidence intervals requires regularity conditions and an appropriate reference distribution. Boundary parameters, small samples and nonidentification can invalidate the usual chi-square approximation. This tutorial prints profile geometry and does not claim calibrated confidence coverage for its noiseless fixture.

A grid can miss a narrow minimum or truncate a broad interval. Expand or refine it based on the scientific scale and observed geometry. Compare nuisance solutions and objective values, not merely the index of the smallest grid point. Weak identification often suggests collecting observations over a broader design range, measuring an additional quantity or reducing the model's ambition.

## Answered review questions

**Can regularization solve identification?** It can select one answer by adding information or preference. It does not make the original data distinguish previously equivalent parameter values. Report which conclusion is driven by the data and which by the regularizer.

**Why can a small gradient be misleading?** A flat valley has small gradients over many parameter values. A tiny score does not establish a unique optimum or precise extrapolation.

**What would a strong modeling interview answer include?** Show the nonidentified parameter combination, explain how the experimental design limits information, describe a valid reparameterization or additional measurement, and distinguish numerical stabilization from a scientific resolution of ambiguity.
