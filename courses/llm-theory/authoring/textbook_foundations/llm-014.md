## Assemble a small experiment whose result can be reconstructed

This capstone is the point where the earlier ideas become one auditable chain. Begin with a declared vocabulary and training documents. Fit a probability table using only their internal transitions. Freeze that table. Score a separate held-out sequence, retaining both its total loss and the number of targets. Finally, write a conclusion whose scope matches those observations. None of these steps requires a large model, but each will remain necessary when the fitted state contains thousands or billions of parameters.

The training documents are ABAB and AC, encoded as [0,1,0,1] and [0,2] under vocabulary order [A,B,C]. The first contributes A to B twice and B to A once; the second contributes A to C once. The count total is four. Add-one smoothing yields A's row [1,3,2]/6, B's row [2,1,1]/4 and C's row [1,1,1]/3. Writing the denominators explicitly makes it possible to detect both missing observations and incorrectly added smoothing mass.

The held-out sequence A,C,B supplies two conditional questions. The first, C after A, receives probability 1/3 from A's fitted row. The second, B after C, also receives 1/3 because C had no outgoing training observations and uses the predefined uniform smoothed row. The total loss is 2*log(3), the mean is log(3), and perplexity is 3. This is a complete derivation of the reported scalar from the original documents. It also identifies the mechanism responsible for the second prediction: a smoothing fallback, not a learned observation of C followed by B.

Saving the count matrix before evaluation and comparing it afterward checks that scoring did not update it. That does not prove that the holdout was never examined during an earlier design choice. The experiment therefore needs a written policy as well as state checks: what data selected the vocabulary and smoothing, what records were reserved, and what was changed after seeing results? A reproducible computation and an independent assessment are complementary properties.

The causal-prefix exercise uses a different small mechanism, cumulative averaging of [2,4,8], producing [2,3,14/3]. Recomputing each prefix should reproduce the corresponding early outputs. This validates the information boundary of that averaging operation. It does not establish that the count model uses attention or that the held-out loss proves causal correctness. The notebook deliberately keeps these observations separate instead of merging several passed checks into one vague success label.

A useful final report can therefore contain both a positive result and a limitation: the count table and scoring rule produced mean loss log(3) on two held-out transitions, and the tested cumulative computation preserved its prefixes. Those are exact local observations. Two targets from one authored sequence do not establish broad predictive quality, and a three-token vocabulary does not resemble the range of ordinary language. The experiment is valuable because a reviewer can see what happened and choose the next informative comparison.
