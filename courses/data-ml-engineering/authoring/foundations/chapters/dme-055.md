# dme-055: System-design interviews grounded in constraints

## Turn an open question into a testable design

A system-design discussion is not a contest to name the most tools. It is a process for translating a product need into workload assumptions, correctness rules, resource constraints, failure behavior and evidence. Two defensible designs can use different technologies if they satisfy the same requirements under different operational contexts. A design becomes weak when its assumptions are hidden or when it silently changes the question to fit a familiar tool.

Begin with the consumer and decision. Who uses the result, how fresh must it be, what latency matters, how much history is required, and what happens when data or a dependency are missing? Define the input and output grain, update semantics and retention boundary. Then estimate workload: records per interval, row size, key distribution, query mix, concurrent demand and growth. Use units and explicit uncertainty rather than a large number without context.

This chapter implements a small constraint evaluator over hypothetical architecture proposals. It is not a benchmark or a real infrastructure recommender. Its numbers are declared design assumptions so the logic can be inspected. The exercise shows how changing requirements or evidence can change the preferred option, and why an unqualified proposal should not become a production claim merely because it looks feasible on paper.

## Hard constraints before preference scores

The proposals are abstract batch, online and hybrid designs. Each has assumed freshness, query latency, retention and cost units. The requirements specify maximum freshness delay, maximum latency, minimum retention and a budget. The evaluator records a reason for every violated constraint and chooses the least expensive feasible proposal, breaking ties by name for determinism.

Let \(F\) be the set of designs satisfying all hard requirements. The selection rule is

\[
d^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{d\in F}\operatorname{cost}(d).
\]

If \(F\) is empty, the correct result is no feasible design under the current assumptions. A low price does not compensate for violating a required retention or latency boundary. A weighted score that lets unrelated strengths cancel a hard constraint can make an unacceptable design look attractive. Preference ranking belongs after the non-negotiable requirements are identified.

Cost units in the example are illustrative, not provider prices. The latency and freshness values are also assumptions, not measurements of a named engine. A real comparison must include deployment, maintenance, incident response, data movement and organizational expertise in addition to a compute bill. The small evaluator keeps those concerns explicit by refusing to label its scenario values as actual qualification.

## Worked case 1: daily reporting and interactive history choose differently

The first scenario needs a daily-style report with up to 60 minutes of freshness delay, up to 10 seconds of query latency, 365 days of retention and a budget of 100 units. The batch proposal assumes 60-minute freshness, eight-second latency, a year of history and cost 20. The hybrid proposal also fits but costs 80. The evaluator selects batch under these declared requirements.

The second scenario changes only freshness and latency: it needs two-minute freshness and 500-millisecond responses while retaining a year of history. Batch fails those timing constraints. The online proposal is fast and fresh but retains only seven days, so it fails the history requirement. Hybrid satisfies all four assumed constraints and becomes the provisional choice.

The program retains the rejection reasons rather than printing only the winner. This is useful in an interview or design review because it shows why an option was excluded and what requirement would need to change for it to become viable. It also prevents an attractive latency number from hiding a retention failure. The same technology label is not universally best; the decision follows the workload and contract.

## Worked case 2: changed assumptions and missing evidence invalidate a choice

The second case revises the hybrid latency assumption from 200 to 800 milliseconds. Under the interactive requirement of 500 milliseconds, no proposal now satisfies every constraint. The evaluator returns no selection. It does not silently raise the latency limit, drop the history requirement or increase the budget to preserve a preferred architecture.

A separate evidence gate also rejects all proposals when actual qualification is required, because every fixture proposal is explicitly unqualified. The Boolean in this small model represents the missing evidence decision; it is not a trusted production verification system. A real release gate needs source-bound test results, runtime identities and validated operational reports, not a caller setting a flag to true.

The next action is to gather the evidence that could change the decision: representative native end-to-end latency, feature or data freshness, and restore behavior under the declared workload. A design review should identify these decisive unknowns early. “We need more testing” is less useful than naming the measurement, its acceptance threshold and the candidate identity it must qualify.

## Structure a complete interview answer

After requirements and workload estimates, sketch the data path at meaningful boundaries: source capture, durable storage, transformation, accepted publication, serving and observability. For each boundary, explain identity, schema, ordering, retry behavior and failure handling. A diagram should show where state lives and who is allowed to change the accepted version, not merely boxes containing product names.

Explain one end-to-end request or record. For a data product, follow an identified delivery through validation, transformation and publication. For an ML system, follow a feature observation through availability-aware retrieval, model evaluation, activation and inference. Use the connected capstones as concrete examples of those paths. A reviewer should be able to locate the acceptance decision and the evidence supporting it.

Then discuss scale and failure. What becomes the bottleneck if volume grows tenfold? What happens when one partition is hot, a source is late, a dependency is unavailable or a worker exits after committing? Which operations are safe to retry, and which require reconciliation? What state must survive container replacement? These questions expose whether the design has operational substance beyond a happy-path drawing.

## Use estimates as hypotheses

A rough size calculation can reveal an impossible proposal. If \(n\) records arrive per day and each requires \(b\) stored bytes before replication and indexes, base daily storage is \(nb\). Retention, replication, metadata and derived artifacts add further terms. State what is included and what is omitted. Compression ratios and query selectivity should come from representative data or be labelled assumptions.

Latency is also a budget across stages. A 500-millisecond objective cannot allocate 500 milliseconds independently to feature retrieval, model inference and serialization. Queueing and network overhead consume part of the same budget. Use the capacity chapter to reason about bursts and tails rather than assuming average service time determines every request's experience.

Do not overfit the design to a toy benchmark. A system may behave differently under skew, concurrent maintenance, cold caches or a degraded dependency. The acceptance plan should include the workload conditions that matter to the product. A design that meets latency only when nothing else is happening may not meet the actual operational requirement.

## Make tradeoffs and uncertainty visible

Every additional service adds interfaces, state, upgrades and operational responsibility. A simpler local or embedded engine may be appropriate for a bounded workload, while a distributed system may be necessary for scale or availability. Explain the threshold that would justify moving from one to the other. Avoid both extremes: adding a cluster without evidence and insisting a single process can meet requirements it cannot support.

Distinguish reversible choices from expensive commitments. A file layout can sometimes be rewritten; an identifier contract or public API may be much harder to change. Preserve stable identities and versioned interfaces where possible. Record alternatives and why they were rejected so future engineers can revisit the decision when the workload or evidence changes.

## Answered interview discussion

If asked, “Which database would you choose?” begin by asking what queries, update semantics, data volume, availability and operational constraints the database must support. Then compare a small set of plausible options against those constraints. If asked, “What if the latency target becomes ten times tighter?” identify which stage dominates and whether the new target changes architecture, precomputation or the product promise.

If asked how to prove the design works, describe independent correctness oracles, actual native runtime tests, service readback, persistence and recovery checks, and representative load evidence. Keep those tiers separate. A source review can establish that a mechanism exists; it cannot establish the latency of an unexecuted deployment.

For an answered extension, relaxing the interactive scenario's history requirement to seven days makes the online proposal feasible and cheaper than hybrid under the fixture's assumptions. That is a legitimate new decision because the requirement changed explicitly. Silently making the same relaxation to force a desired answer would be a design error.
