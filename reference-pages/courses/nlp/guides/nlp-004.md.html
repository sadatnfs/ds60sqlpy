nlp-004: Segmentation, tokenization and subwords#

Level and prerequisites#

Beginner NLP; about two hours. Direct prerequisite: nlp-003. You should understand Unicode strings, code-point offsets, explicit extraction policies and exact comparisons. This chapter builds a small tokenizer so its choices are visible before you encounter a production tokenizer library.

A token is a unit chosen by an algorithm#

A language model does not receive a paragraph as an indivisible meaning. A tokenizer maps the input into a sequence of token identities. Tokens may correspond to words, word fragments, characters, bytes, punctuation or special control symbols. A single visible word can become several tokens, and a token can contain parts of whitespace or punctuation depending on the implementation.

Tokenization affects sequence length, context limits, embedding lookups, training targets and evaluation units. It can change how much of a document fits into a prompt, how rare names are represented and how much compute a particular language requires. It is therefore part of the model contract. Replacing a tokenizer without matching the model's vocabulary and embeddings is not an interchangeable text-cleaning operation.

Distinguish segmentation, which decides boundaries in a text, from vocabulary encoding, which assigns model IDs to the resulting pieces. A special token represents a structural role such as sequence termination or a chat boundary. A subword vocabulary represents many words using reusable smaller units. Out-of-vocabulary behavior specifies what happens when an input cannot be represented by the known inventory.

A tokenizer does not prove linguistic correctness. Different tasks benefit from different boundaries. Splitting a contraction may be acceptable for one statistical representation and inconvenient for a rule that needs the original word. A legal citation, product code, emoji sequence and ordinary sentence can each expose different weaknesses. Preserve raw text and specify the transformations that happened before tokenization.

Worked example: three plausible policies#

import re

text = "can't pay."
print(text.split())
print(re.findall(r"\w+|[^\w\s]", text))
print(re.findall(r"\w+(?:'\w+)*|[^\w\s]", text))

Expected: ["can't", 'pay.'], then ['can', "'", 't', 'pay', '.'], then ["can't", 'pay', '.']. Whitespace splitting keeps punctuation attached. The first regex separates the apostrophe. The second keeps a straight-apostrophe contraction together. None is a universal language tokenizer: the last pattern says nothing about a curly apostrophe, languages without spaces or every punctuation convention.

Ask which boundary matters to the downstream task. For word-level annotation, you may need agreement with the annotation tool. For a pretrained model, you need its exact tokenizer. For a small bag-of-words baseline, a documented simple policy may be sufficient. Choosing the policy after inspecting the final evaluation score without accounting for the selection process can bias the comparison.

finditer can retain raw code-point spans for a regex tokenizer. The match pay occupies [6,9) in this example. If you normalize the text first, those spans refer to the normalized version. A tokenizer library may provide alignment metadata, but its offset convention must still be checked. Do not assume that every token has a nonempty human-visible span: structural markers can be synthetic.

What byte-pair encoding tries to accomplish#

A simple form of byte-pair encoding, or BPE, starts with small symbols and repeatedly merges a frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol. The historical name does not mean every implementation uses bytes as its initial units. The teaching implementation here uses lowercase ASCII characters inside already separated words, plus an explicit end-of-word marker. It is a character-based word BPE demonstration, not the tokenizer of a particular commercial LLM.

Suppose the training corpus contains the word low three times and lower once. Start with ('l','o','w','</w>') and ('l','o','w','e','r','</w>'). The pair ('l','o') appears four times after weighting by word frequency. So does ('o','w'). We resolve this tie lexicographically to make the example reproducible. The first merge creates lo; the next frequent merge combines lo and w into low.

After those two learned merges, low is represented as ('low','</w>') and lower as ('low','e','r','</w>'). Frequent sequences can now use fewer symbols. The greedy merge criterion is a frequency/compression heuristic. It does not optimize truthfulness, sentiment classification or syntactic understanding. A tokenizer can be efficient at representing an unhelpful or biased corpus.

Worked example: merge without overlapping replacements#

def merge_pair(tokens, pair):
    result = []
    i = 0
    while i < len(tokens):
        if i + 1 < len(tokens) and (tokens[i], tokens[i + 1]) == pair:
            result.append(tokens[i] + tokens[i + 1])
            i += 2
        else:
            result.append(tokens[i])
            i += 1
    return tuple(result)


print(merge_pair(("l", "o", "w", "</w>"), ("l", "o")))
print(merge_pair(("a", "a", "a", "</w>"), ("a", "a")))

Expected: ('lo','w','</w>') and ('aa','a','</w>'). The repeated a example matters. Although the original sequence has two adjacent ('a','a') positions, the left-to-right nonoverlapping replacement consumes the first two symbols together and leaves the third. A naive replacement that reuses one symbol twice would not implement this operation.

The loop advances by two after a merge and one otherwise. Each original token is consumed exactly once. That invariant explains why the function preserves order and why decoding the merged symbols can recover the same word. This function operates on a tuple of symbols, not directly on arbitrary prose. The word boundary marker is part of the teaching representation and must be handled separately when decoding.

Worked example: learn and freeze two merges#

from collections import Counter

words = {"low": 3, "lower": 1}
state = {word: tuple(word) + ("</w>",) for word in words}
merges = []
for _ in range(2):
    counts = Counter()
    for word, tokens in state.items():
        for pair in zip(tokens, tokens[1:]):
            counts[pair] += words[word]
    pair = min(counts, key=lambda candidate: (-counts[candidate], candidate))
    merges.append(pair)
    state = {word: merge_pair(tokens, pair) for word, tokens in state.items()}
print(merges)
print(state)

Expected learned pairs: [('l','o'), ('lo','w')]. Expected state: low maps to ('low','</w>'), and lower to ('low','e','r','</w>'). Run this block after defining merge_pair from the preceding example. The Counter is rebuilt after each merge because the available neighboring symbols have changed. Reusing the original counts would train a different and generally incorrect procedure.

(-counts[candidate], candidate) sorts first by descending frequency and then by the pair itself. The explicit tie rule makes the tiny training run deterministic across dictionary insertion orders. A production tokenizer has more implementation details, including normalization, pre-tokenization, reserved IDs, byte handling and merge ordering. Record those details rather than inferring compatibility from the label “BPE.”

When encoding a new word, apply the learned merges in the recorded order. Do not learn new merges from the test document just to reduce its length. That would change the representation being evaluated. For this teaching model, row uses only known characters and remains ('r','o','w','</w>'): the learned rules do not include a standalone ('o','w') merge. Greedy longest-substring matching is not automatically equivalent to applying this learned merge sequence.

Unknown symbols, byte coverage and round trips#

The training alphabet contains l,o,w,e,r. The word slow introduces an unseen s. The reference implementation raises an explicit error. This is intentionally restrictive so the unknown-symbol boundary is visible. Other tokenizers use a dedicated unknown token or a byte-level fallback. Byte-level coverage can represent arbitrary input bytes, but that does not mean every string gets a short sequence or a useful semantic representation.

For supported inputs, a basic invariant is that decoding an encoding returns the original modeled input. In this toy system, concatenating the symbols and removing the single terminal marker recovers the word. The input contract restricts words to lowercase ASCII letters, so the literal marker cannot appear inside a valid input. Do not generalize that decoding rule to arbitrary Unicode text or a production tokenizer's special-token behavior.

A round trip is necessary for some applications but not enough for quality. A character tokenizer can round-trip perfectly while producing long sequences. A preprocessing pipeline may intentionally lose case or accent distinctions before encoding, so decoding returns only the transformed input. State which representation your round-trip claim covers.

From tokens to model inputs#

After segmentation, a fixed vocabulary maps each token to an integer ID. An embedding matrix E has one row per vocabulary entry and a chosen hidden dimension d. Looking up E[token_id] yields a vector of length d; a sequence of L token IDs becomes an L × d array before adding position information or applying attention. The integer ID is an index, not an ordinal measure of meaning.

Special tokens can affect training and chat behavior. An end-of-sequence token can become a prediction target. Padding tokens usually need masks so their positions do not contribute to the intended loss. A chat template can insert role and boundary markers that the model expects. The applied LLM chapters show why a response-only fine-tuning loss must mask the appropriate prompt positions. Tokenization and masking errors can quietly change the optimization objective even when every tensor shape looks valid.

Context budgets and cost estimates must use the actual deployed tokenizer. Character counts, words and bytes are at best approximations. Comparing perplexities from different tokenizations also changes the denominator and event space. Explain the unit before making a model-quality claim, and use an appropriate common-unit comparison when the scientific question requires one.

Run and practice#

Use the Docker python-core profile and courses/nlp/learners/nlp-004.py. The examples use small local strings and do not download a tokenizer or model. Keep the learned merge list in your notes so encoding can be reproduced.

  1. Compare segmentation policies. Reproduce the three token lists for can't pay. and inspect raw spans with finditer. Expected: the lists shown above and pay at [6,9). Verify: each span slices the unchanged input to its reported token.
  2. Count weighted adjacent pairs. Use {'low':3,'lower':1} before any merge. Expected: both ('l','o') and ('o','w') have count 4; ('w','</w>') has count 3. Verify: explain why counting word types alone would give different values.
  3. Handle overlapping candidates. Merge ('a','a') in ('a','a','a','</w>'). Expected: ('aa','a','</w>'). Verify: reconstruct the same three-letter word and show that no symbol was consumed twice.
  4. Train deterministic merges. Learn exactly two merges with the stated tie rule. Expected: ('l','o'), then ('lo','w'), with the stated final tokenizations. Verify: reverse the input dictionary's insertion order and obtain the same result.
  5. Freeze inference behavior. Encode lower and row, then try slow under the restricted training alphabet. Expected: the known words encode and round-trip; slow raises a clear unknown-symbol error. Verify: no new merge is added during encoding.
  6. Connect representation to a model contract. Write a record containing tokenizer type, normalization policy, vocabulary identity, merge order and special-token rules. Expected: a concrete explanation of which changes would require rechecking model compatibility and context budgets. Verify: explain why a successful round trip does not prove semantic quality or interchangeable perplexity units.

Hints, pitfalls and interview reasoning#

First list the symbols and adjacent pairs by hand. Next multiply pair occurrences by each word's frequency. Then apply only the selected pair and rebuild counts. For inference, keep the merge list immutable and trace one rule at a time. If decoding fails, inspect the boundary marker and transformation policy before blaming the embedding model.

Why not use a whole-word vocabulary? It can produce short sequences for frequent known words but must handle rare names, inflections and unseen words. Subwords trade vocabulary size against sequence length and representational flexibility. The tradeoff depends on the corpus and languages.

Does byte coverage solve multilingual quality? It solves a representation-coverage issue. A language may still need more tokens for the same amount of information, receive less useful training exposure or suffer from poor evaluation. Coverage, efficiency and model quality are distinct properties.

Why freeze a tokenizer during an experiment? Its vocabulary, normalization and merge rules define the feature representation and loss units. Changing them mid-comparison changes more than the model weights. Record the version and evaluate deliberate changes under a clear protocol.
