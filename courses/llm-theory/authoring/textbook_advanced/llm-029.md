## Read a scaling curve as an experiment, not a law of nature

The tiny capstone held its architecture, corpus and update budget fixed. A scaling study deliberately varies such quantities to understand how predictive loss changes as resources grow. The difficulty is not drawing a smooth curve. It is identifying which quantities changed, which remained controlled, and which conclusions the design can support. A trend measured within one model/data/training family is evidence about that family, not an unrestricted promise about every larger model.

Use P for the number of model parameters, D for the amount of distinct corpus data, S for optimizer steps, N for the number of target tokens actually processed during training, and C for a declared compute measure. D and N are not necessarily equal. Revisiting the same thousand tokens for ten epochs processes ten thousand token events but does not create ten thousand distinct observations. Likewise, a step count is not a token count unless batch and sequence shapes are fixed.

### Separate optimization progress from model scaling

The worksheet's run A has P=1000,D=10000,S=100,\(C=1\) and loss 2.0. Run B keeps P and D fixed, doubles S and C, and reports loss 1.8. This comparison is consistent with a benefit from additional optimization under those conditions. It does not isolate the effect of increasing parameter count because no parameter-count change occurred. The loss reduction is .2 in the stated unit, not a 20 percent reduction; relative to 2.0 it is 10 percent.

A third row changes P,D,S and C together. If loss falls to 1.5, several explanations remain possible: a more expressive model, more varied data, more repeated processing, different optimization or interactions between them. Saying "doubling parameters caused the gain" would discard those alternatives. A controlled experiment varies one factor while holding the relevant others fixed, or uses a design rich enough to estimate their joint effects with explicit assumptions.

This is particularly important when a published plot shows the best run achieved at each budget. Those points may represent different model sizes, data amounts and schedules selected by an optimization procedure. The curve is then an empirical compute frontier, not the learning curve of one fixed model. Read the axes and selection policy before interpreting its slope.

### Derive what a power-law fit means

A simple illustrative form is \(L(C)=L_{\infty}+AC^{-\alpha}\), where L is loss, L_infinity is an asymptotic floor, A is a positive scale and alpha is a positive exponent. The decreasing term describes excess loss above the fitted floor. If the floor were known, taking logs gives \(\log(L-L_{\infty})=\log A-\alpha\log C\). A straight line on those transformed axes would have slope-alpha.

The floor matters. Suppose loss falls from 2.0 at \(C=1\) to 1.5 at \(C=4\). Ignoring a floor gives a fitted exponent \(\frac{\log(\frac{2}{1.5})}{\log(4)}\), about .208. Assuming a floor of 1.0 instead compares excess losses 1.0 and .5, yielding exponent \(\frac{\log(2)}{\log(4)}=0.5\). Both two-point calculations fit their respective formulas exactly, yet they imply different future behavior. Two observations cannot independently identify a floor, amplitude and exponent.

Even with many points, uncertainty is not removed by taking logarithms. Measurement noise, shared evaluation examples, seed variation and model-selection choices affect the fit. The transformed residuals may have a different variance structure from the original losses. A credible study reports the fitting range, protocol and uncertainty, and checks whether withheld budget points behave as predicted. A visually straight segment does not establish that the same slope continues across several unobserved orders of magnitude.

### A fixed compute budget creates a tradeoff

For an illustrative dense-transformer training calculation, suppose compute is approximated by \(C=k\cdot P\cdot N\), where k summarizes the chosen operation-count convention and architecture assumptions. At fixed C, increasing P leaves fewer processed tokens N. A larger model can therefore receive fewer updates or less repeated exposure. Whether that allocation is better depends on the loss response to both size and data processing, not merely on capacity.

Consider a toy excess-loss model \(A\cdot {P}^{-\alpha}+B\cdot {N}^{-\beta}\). Substituting \(N=\frac{C}{kP}\) gives \(A\cdot {P}^{-\alpha}+B\cdot {\left(\frac{kP}{C}\right)}^{\beta}\). The first term decreases as P grows; the second increases because the fixed budget allows fewer processed tokens. Setting the derivative with respect to P to zero gives \(\alpha\cdot A\cdot {P}^{-\alpha}=\beta\cdot B\cdot {\left(\frac{kP}{C}\right)}^{\beta}\). This condition balances the marginal penalties of the two constrained terms. It is a mathematical consequence of the assumed toy formula, not a fitted recommendation for this course's decoder.

Under that assumed form, P grows proportionally to \({C}^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}\) and N to \({C}^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}\), with constants depending on A,B,k,alpha,beta. Different fitted exponents or modeling assumptions produce different allocations. This explains why scaling recommendations must be tied to their measured regime. The [early scaling-law study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361) and the later [compute-optimal training study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556) are primary examples of empirical investigations, not interchangeable universal constants to paste into a tiny experiment.

### Training-optimal and deployment-optimal are different objectives

A study may minimize pretraining loss for a fixed training budget. A product may instead need to minimize total cost over training and millions of inference requests, or satisfy a latency and memory constraint. A smaller model trained for longer could be attractive for repeated serving even if another allocation lies on a particular training-compute frontier. The objective has changed, so the appropriate optimum can change too.

The memory chapter also showed that compute and memory are separate resources. A model can fit an arithmetic compute budget while exceeding parameter, optimizer-state or cache capacity. Context length changes attention work and the task information available to the model. Data quality, duplication and domain coverage can change what a counted token contributes. A resource table should not treat all token events as equally informative merely because their count is convenient.

For a small local study, choose a narrow question. For example: with a fixed architecture and train-only corpus, what happens when the update budget doubles? Or, with a fixed parameter budget and target events, how do two architectures compare? Preserve source hashes, split identities, counts and actual losses. Run multiple seeds when the question requires stability, and reserve evaluation cases for the intended inference. These steps produce a bounded empirical claim without pretending to reproduce large-scale research.

### Diagnose an apparent scaling failure

If a larger run performs worse, do not assume the scaling relationship was disproved immediately. Check whether it received enough optimization, whether the learning-rate schedule changed effectively, whether padding altered the active-token count, whether a data pipeline repeated rather than expanded the corpus, and whether evaluation used the same tokenizer and targets. Conversely, a favorable loss point does not prove the causal contribution of every changed factor.
