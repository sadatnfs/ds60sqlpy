tf-025: Embedding objectives and negative examples#

Level and prerequisites#

Advanced; approximately 120 minutes. Direct prerequisites: tf-024. The prerequisite sequence introduces the notation used here; revisit its worked examples if an axis, probability or gradient is unfamiliar.

Learning objectives#

Connect embedding similarity to the objective and negative sampling policy. The central investigation is concrete: Use a=[1,0], positive=[1,1] and negative=[0,1]; calculate dot products and cosines, then replace the negative with a duplicate of the positive. The finish condition is Dot products are 1 and 0; cosine similarities are 1/sqrt(2) and 0. A negative identical to the positive is flagged before a contrastive loss is interpreted. A useful explanation connects the calculation to the reason it is needed, rather than treating a function name as a definition.

Purpose and mental model#

An embedding objective encourages relationships between representations. Dot products depend on both direction and magnitude, whereas cosine divides by both vector norms and measures direction. A positive pair is declared related; a negative pair is declared unrelated under a sampling policy. That policy is part of the task. If a duplicate of the positive is labelled negative, the objective asks the model to satisfy contradictory evidence. A numerical loss can still be finite in this situation, so inspect identities before optimizing. The three fixed vectors let you calculate every score and show why a higher dot product is not always stronger directional similarity.

Vocabulary and concepts#

Positive pair: Two examples declared related by the training task.

Negative pair: An example pair declared unrelated under a sampling policy.

Cosine similarity: Dot product divided by the product of vector lengths.

Syntax anatomy and reasoning sequence#

reduce_sum(ab) is a dot product for equal-length vectors. Divide by norm(a)norm(b) for cosine and reject a zero norm. A two-candidate contrastive loss can be written as sparse softmax cross entropy with target index0. Temperature divides scores before normalization. It changes the sharpness of probabilities and the scale of gradients, not which candidate initially ranks first.

Similarity is a modeling decision#

For anchor [1, 0] and positive [1, 1], the dot product is 1 and cosine is 1/sqrt(2). Multiplying the positive by ten raises the dot product to 10 while leaving its direction, and therefore its cosine, unchanged. A retrieval system using dot products can prefer a large-norm candidate for a reason that cosine deliberately removes. Neither score is automatically correct; the choice should match the training objective and intended use.

With candidate scores [1, 0] and the first candidate designated positive, the two-candidate cross entropy is log(1 + exp(-1)). Dividing scores by a positive temperature changes the probability gap. Temperature 0.5 produces scores [2, 0] and a smaller loss for this already correctly ranked pair. The ordering is unchanged, but the derivative with respect to each original score contains a factor of one over temperature. Temperature changes both the probabilities and the gradient scale.

A negative is a sampling claim, not a fact inferred from a different row position. If the negative is another copy of the positive, the model receives contradictory supervision. Compare canonical identities before constructing the candidate set. Identity checks catch known duplicates; two distinct documents can still express the same meaning, so the broader false-negative problem requires examining the sampling policy and data.

Cosine is undefined for a zero vector because its denominator vanishes. Adding an epsilon silently chooses a different behavior near zero. This lesson rejects zero norms explicitly. For a larger system, document how empty, failed or near-zero representations are handled and count them in evaluation instead of dropping them from the reported denominator.

Worked example / walkthrough#

Example A: Compare similarity measures#

The positive is not parallel to the anchor, so its cosine is below one despite a dot product of one. Magnitudes and directions should not be conflated.

import numpy as np
import tensorflow as tf

a=np.array([1.,0.]); pos=np.array([1.,1.]); neg=np.array([0.,1.])
assert (a@pos,a@neg)==(1,0)
np.testing.assert_allclose([(a@pos)/(np.linalg.norm(a)*np.linalg.norm(pos)),a@neg],[1/np.sqrt(2),0],atol=1e-12,rtol=0)

Expected: dots1,0; cosines1/sqrt2,0.

Example B: Scale a candidate#

Scaling changes the unnormalized score but leaves direction unchanged. This demonstrates why normalization is a modeling choice with consequences for retrieval rankings.

import numpy as np
import tensorflow as tf

a=np.array([1.,0.]); pos=np.array([1.,1.]); neg=np.array([0.,1.])
assert (a@pos,a@neg)==(1,0)
np.testing.assert_allclose([(a@pos)/(np.linalg.norm(a)*np.linalg.norm(pos)),a@neg],[1/np.sqrt(2),0],atol=1e-12,rtol=0)
scaled=10*pos
assert a@scaled==10
np.testing.assert_allclose((a@scaled)/(np.linalg.norm(a)*np.linalg.norm(scaled)),1/np.sqrt(2),atol=1e-12,rtol=0)

Expected: dot10; cosine unchanged.

Common pitfalls and diagnosis#

If rankings change after positive rescaling, first check whether the intended score was dot product or cosine. If contrastive loss looks reasonable but duplicate candidates disagree, inspect the identity policy. Check temperature before comparing losses across runs, and reject an undefined cosine instead of treating a finite replacement value as ordinary evidence.
