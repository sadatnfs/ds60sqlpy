## Decide what kind of unfamiliar example the test should represent

A train/test split is meaningful only relative to the generalization question. Predicting another paragraph from a document already seen in training is different from predicting a new document, a new author, a new organization or a future policy revision. Randomly assigning rows can produce a formally disjoint list of IDs while leaving the most relevant information shared across the boundary. The split unit should reflect the intended source of novelty.

The fixture has documents a and a2 in family F, b in G and c in H. The policy assigns F to training, G to validation and H to test. Therefore a and a2 both go to training, even if they are separate files or contain different sentences. The family, not the individual row, is the unit of assignment. This prevents the known relationship between a and a2 from appearing on both sides of the chosen boundary.

Moving only a2 into validation creates the deliberate failure. Training still contains family F through a, and validation now contains F through a2. Their family-set intersection is {F}. The important evidence is that shared identity, not the number of rows involved. Audit every pair of partitions: training versus validation, training versus test, and validation versus test. A clean first comparison does not imply the other two are clean.

Exact content identity answers a narrower question. The strings red square and an identical copy produce the same SHA-256 digest of their UTF-8 bytes. The paraphrase square colored red produces a different digest even though its meaning may be related. A trailing space also changes the raw-byte digest. If both strings are stripped first, their normalized digests can agree. The resulting hash is evidence about the particular bytes or normalized representation supplied to it, not a detector of every semantic relationship.

This motivates retaining several identities rather than searching for one perfect ID. A record ID identifies a stored entry. A raw hash identifies its exact bytes. A normalized hash identifies a declared transformation of those bytes. A family ID records a known source relationship. A split assignment records how the experiment permits that information to be used. Each field has a distinct role, and the audit is stronger when it can explain which relationship triggered an exclusion.

The manifest hash in this chapter binds the sorted assignment dictionary. It detects a change in document-to-partition labels under that serialization rule. It does not automatically bind the document text, tokenizer, model weights or family-construction algorithm. Those require their own recorded artifacts or a manifest that explicitly includes them. A stable hash is useful because it makes a specified change detectable; its strength depends on what was actually included.

The test partition contains one authored document here. That is enough to demonstrate whole-family assignment and a deliberate leak, but not enough to estimate deployment reliability. Unknown relationships can remain even after all known family intersections are empty. A defensible report states the grouping policy, normalization rules, audit results and detection limits, then interprets the held-out score within that scope.
