## Starting point and result

A distributed optimizer can be slower than a serial compiled model on small data. Spark scheduling, serialization, data conversion and repeated scans add fixed and variable costs. This chapter defines a measurement plan that distinguishes those costs instead of presenting a tiny local fixture as proof of massive-data speed.

## Mechanism: decompose the wall-clock budget

For K objective evaluations, a simplified cost model is

\[T\approx T_{\mathrm{prepare}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}(T_{\mathrm{schedule},k}+T_{\mathrm{scan},k}+T_{\mathrm{convert},k}+T_{\mathrm{compute},k}+T_{\mathrm{reduce},k}).\]

Some terms overlap, so this is a diagnostic decomposition rather than an exact additive profiler identity. Compilation and first-use initialization belong to preparation; excluding them is acceptable only when both cold and warm results are labeled. Cache materialization is also work, even if subsequent iterations benefit.

Compare equal objectives and tolerances. A method that stops earlier with a worse gradient norm has not necessarily become faster at the same task. Record rows, features, partitions, parameter dimension, iterations, evaluations, precision, optimizer settings, thread limits, hardware and image/source versions. Report peak memory and spill as well as elapsed time.

## Worked case: small data favors a compiled serial fit

For a few thousand observations and a small parameter vector, an R/TMB or Torch serial fit may finish before Spark has scheduled several passes. The `global-normal-fit` and additive Torch fixtures are correctness references, not promised speedups. Run a warm serial baseline and a warm distributed evaluation over the same fixed parameters before benchmarking a full optimizer.

If the model has sufficient statistics, Spark may compute them once and a local optimizer may then reuse a compact summary. That can be substantially better than repeatedly scanning raw data. Derive the sufficient-statistic property rather than assuming every nonlinear or latent model permits it.

## Worked case: skew defeats nominal parallelism

Suppose ninety percent of observations belong to one indivisible coupled group. Ten workers do not imply a tenfold speedup because the largest group dominates the critical path. More partitions can redistribute independent row contributions, but cannot split the group correctly without a model-specific algorithm. Measure per-task duration and group-size distribution.

An expensive library may also create many threads inside each task. Running several such tasks per executor can oversubscribe CPUs and increase contention. Set and record thread limits for BLAS, Torch and native solvers, then compare throughput under the actual resource cap. More runnable threads are not always more useful computation.

## Failure diagnosis

High scheduler time suggests too many tiny actions or partitions. High conversion time suggests a representation boundary problem. Spill may indicate oversized groups or inappropriate caching. High driver time may come from collecting too much state or a large parameter vector. A slow first evaluation alone can reflect initialization rather than steady-state performance.

## Interview discussion

**How would you prove a speedup?** Use equivalent numerical tasks, explicit warm/cold treatment, representative sizes, repeated measurements and correctness checks. Publish the resource budget and uncertainty in timing rather than one favorable number.

**Can a local two-core result predict a cluster?** It can expose correctness and some bottlenecks, but network, storage, scheduling and skew change at scale. Qualify a real cluster separately before claiming cluster throughput.
