# viz-005: Time series, forecast boundaries and bands

## Level and prerequisites

Intermediate. Direct prerequisites: `viz-003`, `viz-004`, `statistics-027`. Use the prepared Python or JavaScript Plotly variant with inline dates and values.

## A line connects an ordering assumption

Time-series marks need event dates, units and an information boundary. A line implies a meaningful order between observations. Unsorted dates can create a zigzag that is a data-order defect rather than a signal. Missing periods need an explicit gap policy; connecting over a gap can imply unobserved continuity.

The fixture has observed demand 10 through 15 on January 1 through 6. A forecast frozen after January 6 predicts 16,17,18 for January 7 through 9. Actual later observations are 17,16,19. The plot separates history, frozen forecast and later outcomes using labels, markers and line dashes rather than only color.

## Worked case: evaluate the frozen forecast

Squared errors are 1,1,1, so RMSE is one demand unit. A last-value forecast of 15 has squared errors 4,1,16, giving RMSE approximately 2.646. Both procedures must be compared on the same horizon and information boundary. Choosing the forecast after seeing later outcomes would invalidate that comparison even if its line looked impressive.

The reference constructs explicit date strings and a date axis. A date-only series needs a documented timezone interpretation when moved into timestamp data. Do not mix local midnights, UTC instants and categorical date labels without explaining their meaning.

## Contrasting case: a band with no calibration claim

The illustration places a plus/minus two band around forecasts. Upper values are 18,19,20 and lower values 14,15,16. A closed polygon concatenates future dates forward along the upper boundary and backward along the lower boundary. Reversing only one coordinate array twists the polygon and produces misleading fill.

This band is explicitly illustrative. It has no claimed confidence or prediction coverage. A real calibrated forecast interval needs a probabilistic or resampling construction and an evaluation by horizon. Being wide enough to contain three particular outcomes does not establish 95% coverage.

## Six progressive exercises

1. **Trace chronology.** List history and forecast dates separately. Expected evidence: every forecast date follows January 6. Hint: identify the cutoff before drawing the line.
2. **Compute errors.** Calculate model and last-value RMSE. Expected evidence: one versus approximately 2.646 on the same three outcomes. Hint: square before averaging, then take the square root.
3. **Construct the band.** Build the forward-upper/backward-lower polygon. Expected evidence: no crossing and correct lower/upper values at each date. Hint: reverse dates and values together.
4. **Diagnose a gap.** Remove January 4 and explain whether the line should bridge it. Expected evidence: an explicit policy and caption, not a silently fabricated value. Hint: interpolation is a modeling decision.
5. **Distinguish interval types.** Relabel a supplied mean-confidence interval and a supplied outcome-prediction interval correctly. Expected evidence: the caption states which random quantity is covered. Hint: both can be bands but answer different questions.
6. **Transfer to delayed labels.** Suppose each outcome arrives two days later. Identify the latest usable training event at January 6. Expected evidence: January 4, with an availability-time rule. Hint: event time and availability time differ.
