Purpose and mental model#

A sentence follows a path through changing distributions#

A next-token model does not choose an entire sentence from one fixed list of sentence probabilities. It produces a new distribution after each prefix. Think of a branching tree: the root offers first-token alternatives, and each chosen branch leads to another distribution for the next token. Scoring a particular sequence follows one specified path through that tree. Generating a sequence chooses the path as it goes.

The three numbers in the worked example, 0.5, 0.25 and 0.8, belong to three different conditional distributions. The first is the probability of the first scored target under the initial context. The second is the probability of the next observed target after the first has been supplied. The third belongs to the longer observed prefix. There is no requirement that these three numbers sum to one, because they are not competing choices in a single row. Each came from a row whose complete set of candidate probabilities would sum to one.

For a concrete arithmetic trace, retain 50 percent of the initial mass at the first branch, then 25 percent of what remains, then 80 percent of that. The masses are 0.5, 0.125 and 0.1. Multiplication represents nested conditioning: the final path receives one tenth of the initial mass. Using three unconditional token frequencies would describe a different model, one that ignores the changing prefixes. The probability chain rule lets a model express dependence; a chosen restricted-context approximation may then deliberately discard some of it.

Long products are awkward both numerically and conceptually. Negative logarithms turn each factor into a nonnegative contribution that becomes large when the model assigns little probability to the observed target. Here the contributions are log(2)\log(2), log(4)\log(4) and log(1.25)\log(1.25). The second target contributes most because 0.25 is the smallest assigned probability. Their sum is log(10)\log(10), about 2.302585 nats. A nat is the unit obtained with natural logarithms. The mean, about 0.767528 nats per target, answers a different reporting question from the total, even though both come from the same three observations.

This decomposition is diagnostically useful. If a document's score is poor, inspect which target events supplied the large terms and which context each received. A low probability may reflect a rare name, a tokenizer mismatch, a missing context field or a model that has not learned the dependency. The aggregate cannot distinguish those explanations. In the extreme, a probability of zero says the model rules the event out; a positive product that becomes zero only after floating-point multiplication says the representation ran out of range. The log-space example below separates them.

Sequence likelihood is especially useful for comparing predictive models on the same observed targets under the same context, tokenizer and stopping convention. It is less direct as an answer-quality score: there can be several correct answers, and a common plausible answer may be false in the particular case. Likelihood measures how the supplied model allocates probability. The task specification determines which outcomes we want that allocation to support.

A sequence score is a chain of declared events#

For a token sequence x_1 through x_T, the chain rule is Pr(x1,…,xT)=∏t=1TPr(xt∣x1,…,xt−1)\Pr(x_1,\ldots,x_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\Pr(x_t\mid x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1}). This is an identity of probability, not an independence assumption. A model may approximate each conditional using a restricted context, such as only the preceding token, but that restriction is an additional modeling choice. The three numbers in Example A are the probabilities of the three observed targets at their respective contexts. They are not three probabilities from one categorical row and need not sum to one.

Distinguish scoring a full sequence from scoring a suffix. If a prompt u is supplied and held fixed, an answer score is P(y|u), the product of conditionals for answer targets only. Including prompt tokens changes the scored event and denominator. Suppose a prompt contributes a large, easy block of targets while the answer remains difficult. Averaging over prompt and answer can make a model appear better at answering without improving any answer prediction. The scoring policy should say which tokens are context only and which are observations being scored.

A complete finite-string model also needs a termination convention. With an explicit end-of-sequence token EOS, the probability of the exact string includes the conditional probability of stopping after its last content token. Without that factor, the product can describe a prefix that is compatible with many longer strings. Imagine Pr(A∣BOS)=0.5\Pr(A\mid \mathrm{BOS})=0.5 and Pr(EOS∣A)=0.2\Pr(\mathrm{EOS}\mid A)=0.2. The prefix A has probability .5 under this start policy, while the exact one-token terminated string has probability .1. A system that omits EOS cannot use that prefix score as though it were the exact terminated-string probability. The existing fixture supplies three targets directly and does not silently add an EOS event.

Taking a negative logarithm turns multiplication into addition. For [.5,.25,.8], the contributions are log 2, log 4 and log 1.25, summing to log 10. Dividing by three gives a mean in nats per target. A change of logarithm base changes the unit: divide a natural-log score by log 2 to express bits. Probability products are unchanged by that bookkeeping, but comparing numeric loss values across different log bases without conversion is invalid.

Numerical underflow and a genuine zero conditional require different diagnoses. A thousand factors of .01 have a positive exact product 10−2000{10}^{-2000}, but float64 cannot represent that product. Summing their logs still gives about 4605.17 nats. In contrast, a factor exactly zero assigns no probability to the observed event and gives infinite loss under that model. Replacing zero with epsilon changes the model or metric and caps its penalty. Such clipping can be a deliberate engineering policy, but its threshold and resulting interpretation must be disclosed.

Teacher forcing evaluates the conditional at the observed preceding tokens. During generation the model instead conditions on its own sampled or selected previous outputs. These contexts can diverge after the first generated error. Therefore a favorable teacher-forced score is useful evidence about observed-prefix prediction, not a demonstration of every free-generation trajectory. For a masked-attention Transformer, many observed-prefix conditional scores can be computed in one forward pass. That computational parallelism does not make the probability factors independent, nor does it remove the sequential hidden-state dependency of a recurrent model.

Greedy token choice also differs from maximizing a whole sequence. If the first token has probabilities A=0.6A=0.6 and B=0.4B=0.4, and the best second-step continuation has conditional probability .5 after A but .9 after B, the strongest two-token paths have probabilities .30 and .36. Choosing A greedily misses the better complete path. The example fixes a two-step length and ignores stopping solely to isolate this difference; real decoding must also specify length and EOS policies.

Interview reasoning: "Does adding log probabilities discard dependencies?" No. The conditional arguments still contain the declared prefixes. The logarithm changes the arithmetic representation of the product. Dependencies are lost only if the conditional model is replaced with marginals or a shorter context, not because multiplication becomes addition. Check the context supplied to every target before debugging the final sum.

Vocabulary and concepts#

Prefix: the preceding tokens supplied as context before scoring the next target.

Conditional probability: the probability of an event given its stated context, not a claim that successive tokens are independent.

Likelihood: the probability the model assigns to the observed target sequence under its conditions.

Negative log likelihood: the sum of negative natural logarithms of target probabilities; smaller values assign more probability to the observation.

Underflow: a floating-point product becoming zero because its positive mathematical value is too small for the representation.

Syntax or reasoning anatomy#

For probabilities p1, p2 and p3, the product is p1·p2·p3p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\cdot p_{3}. The corresponding total negative log likelihood is −log(p1)−log(p2)−log(p3)-\log(p_{1})-\log(p_{2})-\log(p_{3}). math.log uses the natural logarithm. A mean divides that total by the number of scored target positions, which must be recorded. The logarithm identity improves arithmetic stability; it does not repair probabilities computed from the wrong contexts.

A helper should accept a nonempty list of finite values between zero and one. Reject an invalid negative value, a value above one, and NaN. Treat an explicit zero as infinite negative log likelihood instead of quietly clipping it to an arbitrary positive value. Preserve a finite log-space total even when the separately reported direct product underflows. Do not infer factual correctness or independent evidence from a favorable likelihood.

Worked example / walkthrough#

Run Examples A and B in order in one scratch file, then run that complete file in a fresh process. Example B may reuse the imports, helpers and fixture state established in Example A. Do not treat an isolated later block as a standalone program.

Example A: Trace a three-target score#

Use the supplied conditional table as fixed data, not three independent token marginals.

import math

probabilities = [0.5, 0.25, 0.8]
likelihood = math.prod(probabilities)
nll = -sum(math.log(p) for p in probabilities)
assert math.isclose(likelihood, 0.1, rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-12)
assert math.isclose(nll, math.log(10), rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-12)
print(likelihood, nll)

Expected: The total is log(10)\log(10), while the per-target mean is log(10)3\frac{\log(10)}{3}. A different prefix could change any later conditional; the list is not a universal token-frequency table.

Example B: A finite log score after product underflow#

Repeat a small positive conditional probability many times. Do not replace a zero printed product with a claim that the supplied probabilities were zero.

probabilities = [0.01] * 1000
likelihood = math.prod(probabilities)
nll = -sum(math.log(p) for p in probabilities)
assert likelihood == 0.0
assert math.isfinite(nll)
assert math.isclose(nll, 1000 * math.log(100), rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-9)
print(likelihood, nll)

Expected: The direct product underflows to zero in float64/Python float, but the log-space total remains finite. This is a numeric representation limit, not an impossible event in the declared table.

Exercises#

  1. Trace the fixed conditionals. Derive the product, total negative log likelihood and scored-target count for [0.5,0.25,0.8]. Expected: Likelihood 0.1, total NLL log(10)\log(10), and three targets. Verify: Assert the product and log score within 1e-12 and name each preceding context in a short table.
  1. Change the conditioning assumption. Compare the supplied table with [0.5,0.5,0.5], which incorrectly reuses one unconditional frequency for all positions. Expected: The alternative product is 0.125 and its total NLL is 3·log(2)3\cdot \log(2), different from log(10)\log(10). Verify: Record both products and explain that the changed conditionals define a different model, not an algebraic shortcut.
  1. Preserve a long log score. Implement sequence_score(probabilities) and test 1000 copies of 0.01 without summing logs of an already multiplied product. Expected: Direct product is 0.0 from underflow; total NLL is finite and equals 1000·log(100)1000\cdot \log(100) within 1e-9. Verify: Assert the finite log-space value and keep the underflowed product labeled separately.
  1. Handle certain and impossible targets. Score [1,1] and [0.5,0]. Do not replace zero with an undisclosed epsilon. Expected: The all-one score has NLL 0; a zero-probability target gives product 0 and NLL positive infinity.   1. Reject invalid probabilities. Test an empty list, [-0.1], [1.1] and [NaN]. Expected: All four cases raise ValueError with a nonempty/range/finite explanation. Verify: Capture each exception separately, then assert that the original three-target score still matches log(10)\log(10).

Progressive hints#

  • Hint 1: Write the prefix beside every target probability before multiplying.
  • Hint 2: Take logs of each positive factor before adding; do not log a product that may already be zero.
  • Hint 3: Label whether your denominator counts targets or documents and handle an explicit zero as its own case.

Common pitfalls#

If a product prints zero, inspect the individual probabilities. A thousand positive factors can underflow; a supplied factor of zero represents an impossible observed event under the declared model. Those cases share a printed product but differ in their log score. If an implementation computes log(product)\log(\mathrm{product}) after multiplication, it has already lost the stability benefit.

If two loss reports disagree, inspect whether one is a sequence total, token mean or equal-document mean. A changed tokenizer changes which targets are counted, so a token-based score cannot be casually compared across different representations. High sequence likelihood is also not an answer-verification method: it measures the model's assigned probability to text, not the truth of its content.
