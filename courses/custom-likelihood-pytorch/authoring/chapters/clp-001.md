## The observation comes before the optimizer

A likelihood is a proposed probability law for the observations, viewed as a function of unknown parameters after the observations have been recorded. It is not a probability distribution over those parameters. In a binary experiment, a parameter \(p\) describes a success probability, while \(y_i\) is the recorded outcome. Maximizing the likelihood asks which allowed value of \(p\) makes these particular outcomes most plausible under the proposed experiment. It does not establish that the experiment was sampled without bias or that observations are independent.

This distinction determines what can be parallelized later. If rows describe independent trials conditional on \(p\), their probability factors. If several rows describe one subject with a shared unobserved propensity, multiplying marginal row probabilities changes the model. A Spark partition boundary has no scientific authority to decide which factorization is correct. Write down what was sampled, what was measured, which variables are conditioned upon and where shared randomness enters before writing a reduction.

## Derive the objective

For independent Bernoulli observations, with \(0<p<1\),

\[
L(p;y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{y_i}(1-p)^{1-y_i},\qquad
\mathcal L(p)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{y_i\log p+(1-y_i)\log(1-p)\}.
\]

Here \(L\) is the likelihood and \(\mathcal L\) is the negative log-likelihood, abbreviated NLL. We minimize NLL because logarithms turn products into sums and preserve ordering. The derivative is

\[
\frac{d\mathcal L}{dp}=-\frac{\sum_i y_i}{p}+\frac{n-\sum_i y_i}{1-p}.
\]

For a sample containing both outcomes, setting this derivative to zero gives \(\widehat p=\overline y\). All-success or all-failure samples have boundary optima, a distinction we revisit in logistic regression. The interior restriction in this first program avoids undefined endpoint logarithms; it is a teaching domain, not a claim that boundaries are impossible.

## Worked case 1: three genuinely independent trials

Use observations \((1,1,0)\) and evaluate \(p=0.4\). The product probability is \(0.4^2(0.6)=0.096\), so NLL is approximately \(2.34340709\). The derivative is \(-2/0.4+1/0.6=-10/3\). Its negative sign says a small increase in \(p\) decreases NLL at this point. The complete program checks the handwritten logarithm against `torch.distributions.Bernoulli.log_prob` and checks automatic differentiation against the analytic derivative.

Those are complementary checks. The distribution call verifies density arithmetic and the analytic score verifies differentiation. Neither alone validates the sampling assumptions. The three values are a fixed synthetic fixture, not a dataset from which to make a real-world accuracy claim. Float64 reduces numerical noise in the later derivative comparisons; it does not repair a wrong probability model.

## Worked case 2: two measurements from one latent subject

Suppose a subject has success probability 0.2 or 0.8, each with probability one half, and two measurements are conditionally independent given that subject's propensity. Each marginal measurement has success probability 0.5. However,

\[
P(Y_1=1,Y_2=1)=\tfrac12(0.2)^2+\tfrac12(0.8)^2=0.34,
\]

whereas multiplying the marginal probabilities gives \(0.5^2=0.25\). The shared latent variable creates positive dependence. The correct independent unit is the subject, and the sum or integral over its latent propensity belongs outside the within-subject product. The program evaluates both expressions so that the modeling error is visible without an optimizer.

## Debugging and assumptions

Reject empty data, nonbinary outcomes and probabilities outside the chosen interior domain. Keep the loss as a tensor until gradients have been obtained; converting a parameter-dependent expression to a Python float inside the objective destroys the derivative path. Name your loss reduction explicitly. A row mean and a sum have the same unpenalized optimum in this example but different curvature scales. When observations are dependent, independent-row standard errors generally have the wrong sampling interpretation even if the fitted mean appears reasonable.

## Answered questions a model reviewer should ask

**Is a high likelihood evidence that the scientific model is true?** No. Likelihood compares parameter values within a specified family. Model criticism needs residual checks, predictive checks, alternative models and scrutiny of data collection.

**Can an arbitrary loss be called a likelihood?** Only if it corresponds to a normalized probability model or an explicitly qualified likelihood construction. Adding a penalty changes the estimation problem. A loss can be useful without being a likelihood, but uncertainty formulas must match that choice.

**What should be sent to a distributed worker?** An immutable parameter value and complete independent units, together with the exact objective and reduction contract. This chapter executes one CPU process only; it establishes the mathematical contract that later distributed code must preserve.
