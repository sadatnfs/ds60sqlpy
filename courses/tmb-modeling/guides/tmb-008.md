# Hessian, curvature and weak identification

Start with: tmb-007, statistics-008.

Outcome: Explain when inverse curvature can and cannot support standard errors.

**Where this runs.** In the classroom, the base-R companion at the end of this chapter compares eigenvalues of ordinary and nearly collinear cross-product matrices and applies the delta method. In the separately prepared [owned project](../projects/tmb-local/README.md), `tests/acceptance.R` calls `TMB::sdreport` and requires a positive-definite Hessian (`pdHess`), and `fit_objective()` uses `stats::optimHess` for its optional Newton correction; a companion run is not a TMB compiler or rpy2 receipt, so its output is not evidence that a compiled model or the rpy2 bridge ran.

The Hessian \(H\) contains second derivatives of the negative log likelihood. Near a regular interior optimum, a quadratic approximation is \(f(\theta)\approx f(\hat\theta)+\frac12(\theta-\hat\theta)^TH(\theta-\hat\theta)\). Under suitable sampling assumptions, the inverse observed information \(H^{-1}\) approximates covariance. This is a local asymptotic statement, not a universal uncertainty formula.

For Gaussian regression with known variance \(\sigma^2\), the coefficient information is \(X^TX/\sigma^2\). If columns of \(X\) are linearly dependent, there are parameter directions that do not change the mean and hence do not change the likelihood. The Hessian is singular. More optimizer iterations cannot create information in those directions.

## Key terms

- **Hessian:** The matrix of second derivatives of the negative log likelihood, describing local curvature at the optimum.
- **Observed information:** The Hessian evaluated at the estimate; near a regular interior optimum its inverse approximates the estimator's covariance.
- **Eigenvalue of the Hessian:** The curvature along one principal direction; a near-zero eigenvalue marks a direction where the likelihood barely changes and standard errors explode.
- **Collinearity:** Near linear dependence among design columns, which makes \(X^TX\) nearly singular so the data cannot separate the affected coefficients.

### Worked case A: ordinary and almost duplicate predictors

The companion compares a centered predictor to a second predictor that is almost its duplicate. The smallest eigenvalue of the information matrix approaches zero while the condition number grows. Individual coefficients become unstable even when fitted means remain precise over the observed design. Centering can improve intercept-slope conditioning, but it does not remove actual duplicate information.

A Hessian with one negative eigenvalue at a proposed solution indicates a saddle, numerical error or an invalid approximation. A positive determinant alone does not prove positive definiteness: a two-dimensional matrix with both eigenvalues negative has a positive determinant. Use a Cholesky factorization or inspect eigenvalues for small teaching problems.

### Worked case B: transforming uncertainty

Suppose the estimated log scale is \(\hat s\) with variance \(v_s\). The delta method gives \(\operatorname{Var}(e^{\hat s})\approx e^{2\hat s}v_s\). A symmetric interval on log scale, transformed back by exponentiation, remains positive and is generally asymmetric on the original scale. The companion compares these reports. They are approximations, not identical intervals.

For large random-effect models, the latent precision matrix can be sparse. Explicitly inverting it into a dense matrix can consume far more memory than fitting the model. Request only the quantities needed for a scientific report. `sdreport` can avoid the full report covariance with `getReportCovariance=FALSE`; that does not eliminate every potentially expensive covariance calculation.

### Diagnostics and answered discussion

**Does `pdHess=TRUE` prove the model fits?** No. It concerns local curvature, not residual structure, selection bias or the correctness of the likelihood. **What if a variance is estimated near zero?** Regular Wald intervals can fail at boundaries. Use scientifically appropriate profiles, simulation or alternative inference and explain the boundary. **Can I use a robust sandwich covariance?** Sometimes, under a justified estimating-equation and dependence structure. It is not automatically what `sdreport` returns.

Read the [sdreport implementation and documentation](https://github.com/kaskr/adcomp/blob/master/TMB/R/sdreport.R). Later chapters distinguish fixed-effect information, conditional latent uncertainty and uncertainty of future observations.

### Worked case C: the Gaussian Hessian in closed form

For the normal model in \((\mu,s)\) coordinates, the second derivatives are

\[
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\mu^2}=ne^{-2s},\qquad
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\mu\,\partial s}=2e^{-2s}\sum_i(y_i-\mu),\qquad
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial s^2}=2e^{-2s}S.
\]

At the estimate for \(y=(1,2,4,7)\), the cross term vanishes because residuals sum to zero, \(e^{-2\hat s}=1/5.25\) and \(S=21\). So \(H=\operatorname{diag}(4/5.25,\;2\cdot21/5.25)=\operatorname{diag}(0.7619,\;8)\). The second entry is always \(2n\) at the MLE, since \(S e^{-2\hat s}=n\).

```r
y <- c(1, 2, 4, 7)
fn <- function(p) -sum(dnorm(y, p[1], exp(p[2]), log = TRUE))
p_hat <- c(mean(y), 0.5 * log(mean((y - mean(y))^2)))
H <- optimHess(p_hat, fn)
print(round(H, 4))
se <- sqrt(diag(solve(H)))
print(c(se_mu = se[1], se_log_sigma = se[2], se_sigma_delta = exp(p_hat[2]) * se[2]))
```

The closed form predicts these values, which `optimHess` should reproduce to roughly four decimals because it differentiates numerically:

```text
H = [0.7619  0.0000]      se_mu          = sqrt(5.25 / 4)     = 1.1456
    [0.0000  8.0000]      se_log_sigma   = sqrt(1 / 8)        = 0.3536
                          se_sigma_delta = 2.2913 * 0.3536    = 0.8101
```

Reading the output: \(\operatorname{se}(\hat\mu)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt n\) is the familiar standard error, but with the ML scale \(\sqrt{5.25}\) rather than `sd(y)`. The log-scale standard error \(1/\sqrt{2n}\) does not depend on the data at all, only on the sample size, so four observations always give about 0.354. Converting to \(\sigma\) with the delta method yields 0.81, a symmetric interval \(2.29\pm1.59\) that is uncomfortably close to zero, while exponentiating the log-scale interval gives the positive, asymmetric interval \(\exp(0.829\pm0.693)\approx(1.15,4.58)\). With \(n=4\), the asymptotic approximation is rough either way; the point is how the parameterization changes the reported shape.

### Two curvature traps on small matrices

A Hessian computed numerically at a point that is not the optimum can carry a nonzero cross term; recompute at the returned estimate rather than at the start. Second, sign checks need eigenvalues, not determinants: \(\operatorname{diag}(-1,-2)\) has determinant 2 yet is a local maximum. In the collinear companion matrix, the ordinary design gives \(X^TX=\operatorname{diag}(20,7.368)\) because the centered grid has \(\sum x_i^2=7.368\), a condition number under 3. The nearly duplicated column adds a third direction whose eigenvalue is of order \(10^{-10}\) times the others, so the condition number exceeds \(10^8\) and any standard error in that direction is effectively unbounded.

### Base-R companion (runs in the classroom)

```r
x <- seq(-1, 1, length.out = 20)
X <- cbind(1, x)
near <- cbind(1, x, x + 1e-5 * cos(seq_along(x)))
print(list(
  ordinary = eigen(crossprod(X), symmetric = TRUE)$values,
  nearly_collinear = eigen(crossprod(near), symmetric = TRUE)$values
))
stopifnot(kappa(crossprod(near)) > 1e8)
s <- log(2); se_s <- .2
print(list(delta_sd = exp(s) * se_s, transformed_interval = exp(s + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se_s)))
stopifnot(all(exp(s + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se_s) > 0))
```
