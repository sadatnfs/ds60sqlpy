# The full Gaussian negative log likelihood

Start with: tmb-002, statistics-011.

Outcome: Derive and check the normalized Gaussian objective and its scale estimate.

**Where this runs.** In the classroom, the base-R companion at the end of this chapter evaluates the full Gaussian negative log likelihood and checks it against `dnorm` and against duplicated data. The compiled version is `models/normal_mean.cpp` in the separately prepared [owned project](../projects/tmb-local/README.md), and its `tests/acceptance.R` compares `obj$fn` with the same base-R sum of `dnorm` terms; a companion run is not a TMB compiler or rpy2 receipt, so its output is not evidence that a compiled model or the rpy2 bridge ran.

A normal density is \(p(y\mid\mu,\sigma)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\exp[-(y-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)]\). Independent observations multiply their densities. Taking the negative logarithm turns the product into a sum:

\[
f(\mu,s)=\frac n2\log(2\pi)+ns+\frac12e^{-2s}\sum_i(y_i-\mu)^2,\qquad s=\log\sigma.
\]

Every term matters when estimating the scale. Omitting \(ns\) lets the objective approach zero by sending the scale to infinity. Omitting only a parameter-independent constant preserves the optimizer but changes absolute likelihood comparisons and integration checks. Our models retain normalizers so independent numerical oracles can compare actual density values.

## Key terms

- **Log density:** The logarithm of a probability density at an observation; independent observations add their log densities instead of multiplying densities.
- **Negative log likelihood:** Minus the summed log density of the data, the quantity a TMB objective returns and the optimizer minimizes.
- **Normalization term:** A part of the density, such as \(n\log\sigma\) or \(\frac n2\log(2\pi)\), that makes it integrate to one; dropping the scale term breaks scale estimation, while a parameter-free constant only shifts absolute values.
- **Maximum likelihood scale:** The \(\sigma\) that minimizes the Gaussian NLL, the root mean squared deviation with divisor \(n\), which is smaller than the sample standard deviation with divisor \(n-1\).

### Worked case A: ML scale versus sample SD

For 1, 2, 4 and 7, the mean is 3.5 and the residual sum of squares is 21. The maximum-likelihood variance is \(21/4=5.25\). R's `sd` instead computes \(\sqrt{21/3}\), the square root of the unbiased sample-variance estimator. They answer different questions. The ML estimate comes from differentiating the likelihood jointly in mean and scale. It is not a numerical optimizer error when it differs from `sd`.

```cpp fragment
Type sigma = exp(log_sigma);
nll -= dnorm(y(i), mu, sigma, true);
```

The final `true` requests a log density. Subtraction converts log likelihood to negative log likelihood. Summing tiny ordinary densities or taking their product would cause avoidable floating-point underflow. The companion evaluates the expanded formula and `-sum(dnorm(..., log=TRUE))` independently.

### Worked case B: duplicated observations

Repeat every observation three times. The fitted mean and ML scale stay the same, while the total NLL and information triple. The data are only genuinely more informative if the new observations are independent. Copying rows does not create scientific replication. This distinction matters in Spark: a repeated partition or retry counted twice changes the statistical experiment even when fitted point estimates happen to remain unchanged.

A constant dataset has residual sum of squares zero. The unconstrained Gaussian scale MLE is then on the boundary \(\sigma\to0\), and the likelihood is unbounded. A log transform guarantees positivity but cannot create a finite interior optimum. Treat this as a model/data issue. If measurement precision supplies a defensible lower bound, state it; do not silently clamp the estimate to make a test pass.

### Diagnostics and answered discussion

**May I minimize mean NLL?** For an unpenalized fixed-size problem it has the same optimizer, but gradients, Hessians and regularization scaling change. Inverse curvature from a mean loss must be converted back to total-information scale. **Does normality of residuals guarantee independence?** No. Temporal correlation and group effects require explicit modeling or suitable robust inference. **Why include the constant if it does not affect the mean?** It supports normalized-density checks and consistent marginal likelihood calculations.

The original `normal_mean.cpp` and `tests/acceptance.R` compare the compiled objective to this closed form. A companion R calculation alone is mathematical evidence, not proof that a DLL compiled or ran.

### Worked case C: profiling the scale term by term

Fix the mean at its estimate 3.5. With \(n=4\) and \(S=21\), the objective as a function of \(s=\log\sigma\) is

\[
f(s)=2\log(2\pi)+4s+10.5\,e^{-2s}.
\]

Its derivative \(4-21e^{-2s}\) vanishes at \(e^{2s}=21/4=5.25\), so \(\hat s=\tfrac12\log5.25\approx0.829\). The code below tabulates the full objective and the version with the \(ns\) term dropped.

```r
y <- c(1, 2, 4, 7)
S <- sum((y - mean(y))^2)
s <- c(0, 0.5 * log(S / length(y)), 2)
full <- length(y) / 2 * log(2 * pi) + length(y) * s + S / 2 * exp(-2 * s)
no_log_term <- S / 2 * exp(-2 * s)
print(data.frame(s, full, no_log_term))
```

The values follow directly from the formula, shown rounded as R prints them: \(2\log(2\pi)\approx3.6758\), and the quadratic part \(10.5e^{-2s}\) equals 10.5, 2.0 and about 0.192 at the three points.

```text
          s      full no_log_term
1 0.0000000 14.175754  10.5000000
2 0.8291140  8.992210   2.0000000
3 2.0000000 11.868068   0.1923142
```

The full objective is lowest in the middle row and rises on both sides, which is the profile shape you want around an interior estimate. The truncated column keeps decreasing as \(s\) grows; minimizing it would push \(\sigma\) toward infinity, exactly the failure described above. Notice that at the optimum the quadratic part equals \(n/2=2\) regardless of the data, because \(S/(2\hat\sigma^2)=S/(2S/n)\). That identity gives a quick check of any fitted Gaussian model: at the MLE, the normalized objective equals \(\frac n2\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2)+\frac n2\), here \(2\log(2\pi\cdot5.25)+2\approx8.9922\).

### Where the Gaussian objective goes wrong in practice

Three mistakes recur when this model is written by hand in C++. First, passing the variance where `dnorm` expects a standard deviation: `dnorm(y(i), mu, sigma * sigma, true)` silently fits \(\sigma^2\) as if it were \(\sigma\), and the reported `ADREPORT(sigma)` is then the square root of the true scale. Second, writing `nll += dnorm(..., true)` instead of `-=`, which turns minimization into maximization of the negative likelihood and sends the optimizer to a boundary. Third, forgetting the `true` flag, which returns a density instead of a log density; for observations far from the mean the density underflows to zero and the logarithm taken later is \(-\infty\). Each of these is caught by comparing `obj$fn` with `-sum(dnorm(y, mu, sigma, log = TRUE))` at one or two parameter values, the test `tests/acceptance.R` performs before any fitting.

A useful extra check is scale invariance. Multiply every observation by 10 and the ML scale becomes \(10\sqrt{5.25}\approx22.9\), the mean becomes 35, and the minimum objective increases by exactly \(n\log10\approx9.21\). If a modified template does not show that shift, a normalizing term is missing.

### Base-R companion (runs in the classroom)

```r
y <- c(1, 2, 4, 7)
mu <- mean(y)
sigma <- sqrt(mean((y - mu)^2))
nll <- function(y, mu, sigma) length(y) * log(sigma * sqrt(2 * pi)) + sum((y - mu)^2) / (2 * sigma^2)
print(c(mean = mu, ml_sd = sigma, sample_sd = sd(y)))
stopifnot(abs(nll(y, mu, sigma) + sum(dnorm(y, mu, sigma, log = TRUE))) < 1e-12)
y3 <- rep(y, 3)
stopifnot(mean(y3) == mu, abs(nll(y3, mu, sigma) - 3 * nll(y, mu, sigma)) < 1e-10)
print(c(original_nll = nll(y, mu, sigma), duplicated_nll = nll(y3, mu, sigma)))
```
