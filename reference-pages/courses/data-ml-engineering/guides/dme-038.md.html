dme-038: Temporal evaluation, calibration and decision uncertainty#

Level: Advanced | Prepared runtime: data-engineering

Direct prerequisites: dme-037.

Outcome: Evaluate a realistic future or grouped cohort with calibrated metrics.

Open the worked program in the classroom code pad. This tutorial exposes complete examples and answered discussions without an exam gate. The pad has fresh temporary state; separately documented projects use their own owned lifecycle.

Evaluate the population the system will actually face#

An evaluation split defines a prediction problem. Holding out random rows asks how a procedure behaves on those held-out rows under the resulting information overlap. Holding out entire entities asks how it behaves on entities absent from training. Holding out a later period asks a temporal question. These are not interchangeable estimates of one universal notion of accuracy.

Before selecting a splitting function, identify the deployment population and the information available for each prediction. Will future requests concern known customers or new customers? Can their earlier outcomes legitimately be used? How long do labels take to become observable? A pipeline can fit preprocessing on training rows correctly and still answer the wrong evaluation question.

This chapter executes two investigations. The first contrasts row-level and entity-disjoint evaluation using a real fitted classifier. The second fits a real probability calibrator on an earlier mature-label population and evaluates it on a separate later population. Its base score is deliberately constructed and distorted to make the probability problem visible.

Separate ranking, probability meaning and decisions#

A ranking metric asks whether positive outcomes tend to receive larger scores than negatives. Calibration asks whether a score's probability interpretation agrees with observed frequencies in an appropriate population. A decision rule adds consequences, capacity and an operating threshold. Improvement in one property does not settle the others.

Worked case 1: A perfect row score does not generalize to new entities#

example_one() constructs eighty entities with four rows per entity. Each entity has a fixed synthetic binary outcome, and the only input is its identifier. A one-hot encoder and logistic classifier can learn the outcomes of identifiers they have seen. This is deliberately an easy memorization problem.

The random row split shares 54 entities between training and testing and achieves accuracy one on its held-out rows. The group-aware split holds out entire entities, verifies that no entity appears on both sides and obtains accuracy 0.4. Both investigations use actual fitted models rather than a hand-assigned metric.

The group score addresses the declared target of previously unseen entities. Their identifiers cannot supply the memorized information available for known identifiers. The row score is therefore unsuitable as the reported estimate for that target population. A successful preprocessing pipeline alone does not resolve this mismatch.

This does not establish that every repeated-entity split is invalid. A system intentionally serving known entities with legitimately available history poses a different problem. It still needs appropriate temporal availability and label rules. Define the intended generalization question before turning a preferred split function into a universal rule.

Calibrate on a separate, eligible population#

The second investigation generates a synthetic signal and outcome process over two thousand ordered time steps. A fixed scoring function deliberately stretches and shifts the underlying log odds. Its probabilities are therefore a useful controlled example of poor probability interpretation.

The calibrator is a fitted logistic model of the raw logit zzz:

The parameters aaa and bbb are learned from calibration rows, not final evaluation rows. This is an additional fitted model, so it needs its own separation from evaluation. In the executed fixture the estimated slope is approximately 0.34948 and the intercept approximately -0.50193.

Calibration uses 370 rows from time steps 600 through 969. Their labels are available by the declared calibration cutoff of 1000. Final evaluation uses 600 different rows from time steps 1200 through 1799, with labels fully available by the later reporting cutoff of 1900. The program explicitly checks those chronological and availability relationships.

Worked case 2: Probability scores improve while ranking stays the same#

On the independent later population, raw Brier score is approximately 0.23646 and calibrated Brier score is approximately 0.19860. Raw log loss is approximately 0.78879 and calibrated log loss approximately 0.58453. The actual raw and calibrated AUC values are both approximately 0.70713.

The positive fitted calibration slope preserves score ordering. Probability assignments change while the ranking metric stays unchanged. This is a concrete reason that AUC cannot determine whether a score is suitable as a probability for the cost calculation in chapter 033.

The Plotly output uses five declared probability bins, omitting empty bins. Each empirical point has an accessible row count and coordinates. In the highest calibrated bin there are only nine rows: mean predicted probability is approximately 0.83664 and observed positive fraction is two thirds. Those nine observations do not justify a precise population claim by themselves.

For independent representative Bernoulli observations, a common approximate standard error for a proportion is q^b(1−q^b)/nb\sqrt{\hat q_b(1-\hat q_b)/n_b}q^​b​(1−q^​b​)/nb​​. It explains why small bins are unstable. Small-sample intervals, repeated entities, dependence and multiple comparisons require more care than treating every plotted distance as a significant defect. The program supplies counts and observations; it does not fabricate confidence intervals or assert perfect calibration.

The same case also examines label maturity. Positive outcomes are delayed thirty time steps and negative outcomes one step. At time 1000, the recent fifty-row population contains 38 known labels and twelve pending labels. The known subset has positive fraction approximately 0.10526. The eventual complete synthetic population has fraction 0.32.

The eventual fraction is explicitly a retrospective simulation oracle. It would not be known to an operating system at the earlier cutoff. Using only the fast-arriving outcomes changes the observed population; treating pending outcomes as negative would introduce another error. The program preserves pending counts rather than silently inventing labels.

Complete worked program#

"""Actual grouped evaluation, independent calibration and label-maturity cohorts."""

import json

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, brier_score_loss, log_loss, roc_auc_score
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupShuffleSplit
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder

from ds60sqlpy.plot_artifacts import publish_report


def example_one():
    entities = np.repeat(np.arange(80), 4)
    entity_labels = np.array([0, 1] * 40)
    labels = entity_labels[entities]
    frame = pd.DataFrame({"entity": [f"entity-{value:03}" for value in entities]})
    order = np.random.default_rng(2038).permutation(len(frame))
    random_train, random_test = order[:240], order[240:]
    grouped_train, grouped_test = next(
        GroupShuffleSplit(n_splits=1, test_size=0.25, random_state=38).split(
            frame, labels, groups=entities
        )
    )
    assert not set(entities[grouped_train]) & set(entities[grouped_test])
    assert set(entities[random_train]) & set(entities[random_test])

    def evaluate(train, test):
        model = make_pipeline(
            OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), LogisticRegression(C=10000, random_state=38)
        )
        model.fit(frame.iloc[train], labels[train])
        return float(accuracy_score(labels[test], model.predict(frame.iloc[test])))

    row_score = evaluate(random_train, random_test)
    group_score = evaluate(grouped_train, grouped_test)
    assert row_score > 0.9 and row_score > group_score + 0.2
    return {
        "row_random_accuracy": row_score,
        "unseen_entity_accuracy": group_score,
        "row_random_shared_entities": len(set(entities[random_train]) & set(entities[random_test])),
        "grouped_shared_entities": 0,
        "target_population_for_group_score": "Previously unseen entities",
    }


def reliability(labels, probabilities, family):
    bins = np.digitize(probabilities, np.linspace(0, 1, 6)[1:-1])
    result = []
    for bucket in range(5):
        selected = bins == bucket
        if selected.any():
            result.append(
                {
                    "family": family,
                    "bin": bucket,
                    "rows": int(selected.sum()),
                    "mean_probability": float(probabilities[selected].mean()),
                    "positive_fraction": float(labels[selected].mean()),
                }
            )
    assert sum(row["rows"] for row in result) == len(labels)
    return result


def example_two():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3038)
    time = np.arange(2000)
    signal = rng.normal(size=len(time))
    true_probability = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(signal - 0.7)))
    labels = (rng.random(len(time)) < true_probability).astype(int)
    # Deliberately distorted score: calibration uses independent mature labels.
    raw_logit = 2.8 * (signal - 0.7) + 0.9
    raw_probability = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-raw_logit))
    label_available = time + np.where(labels == 1, 30, 1)
    calibration_rows = (time >= 600) & (time < 970) & (label_available <= 1000)
    future_rows = (time >= 1200) & (time < 1800)
    assert label_available[calibration_rows].max() <= 1000
    assert time[future_rows].min() > time[calibration_rows].max()
    assert label_available[future_rows].max() <= 1900
    calibrator = LogisticRegression(C=1000, random_state=38)
    calibrator.fit(raw_logit[calibration_rows, None], labels[calibration_rows])
    calibrated = calibrator.predict_proba(raw_logit[future_rows, None])[:, 1]
    actual, raw = labels[future_rows], raw_probability[future_rows]
    metrics = {
        "raw_brier": float(brier_score_loss(actual, raw)),
        "calibrated_brier": float(brier_score_loss(actual, calibrated)),
        "raw_log_loss": float(log_loss(actual, raw, labels=[0, 1])),
        "calibrated_log_loss": float(log_loss(actual, calibrated, labels=[0, 1])),
        "raw_auc": float(roc_auc_score(actual, raw)),
        "calibrated_auc": float(roc_auc_score(actual, calibrated)),
    }
    assert np.isclose(metrics["raw_brier"], np.mean((raw - actual) ** 2))
    assert all(np.isfinite(value) for value in metrics.values())
    assert calibrator.coef_[0, 0] > 0
    assert np.isclose(metrics["raw_auc"], metrics["calibrated_auc"])
    recent = (time >= 950) & (time < 1000)
    known_recent = recent & (label_available <= 1000)
    pending = int(np.sum(recent & (label_available > 1000)))
    assert pending > 0 and known_recent.sum() < recent.sum()
    rows = reliability(actual, raw, "Raw score") + reliability(
        actual, calibrated, "Calibrated score"
    )
    return {
        "metrics": metrics,
        "calibration_parameters": {
            "slope": float(calibrator.coef_[0, 0]),
            "intercept": float(calibrator.intercept_[0]),
        },
        "reliability": rows,
        "calibration_rows": int(calibration_rows.sum()),
        "future_evaluation_rows": int(future_rows.sum()),
        "recent_total": int(recent.sum()),
        "recent_known": int(known_recent.sum()),
        "recent_pending": pending,
        "observed_recent_positive_fraction": float(labels[known_recent].mean()),
        "eventual_recent_positive_fraction_simulation_oracle": float(labels[recent].mean()),
        "pending_labels_were_not_replaced_with_zero": True,
    }


def main():
    first, second = example_one(), example_two()
    figure = go.Figure()
    for family in ("Raw score", "Calibrated score"):
        rows = [row for row in second["reliability"] if row["family"] == family]
        figure.add_trace(
            go.Scatter(
                x=[r["mean_probability"] for r in rows],
                y=[r["positive_fraction"] for r in rows],
                mode="lines+markers",
                name=family,
            )
        )
    figure.add_trace(
        go.Scatter(x=[0, 1], y=[0, 1], mode="lines", name="Reference: y = x", line={"dash": "dot"})
    )
    columns = ["family", "bin", "rows", "mean_probability", "positive_fraction"]
    figure.update_layout(
        title="Independent future evaluation with mature labels",
        xaxis_title="Mean predicted probability in a nonempty bin",
        yaxis_title="Observed positive fraction",
        meta={
            "description": "Reliability and row counts; y=x is a reference, not a sample.",
            "table": {
                "columns": columns,
                "rows": [[r[c] for c in columns] for r in second["reliability"]],
            },
        },
    )
    figure.show()
    publish_report(
        {"case_one": first, "case_two": second},
        description="Group-disjoint evaluation, calibration and delayed-label populations",
    )
    print(json.dumps({"case_one": first, "case_two": second}, allow_nan=False))
    print("dme-038: two evaluation cases passed")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Diagnose uncertainty before accepting a model#

A later test period is helpful only when feature availability, fitted transformations and label eligibility also match the intended decision. A random split can obscure new-entity performance. A recent metric can move because reporting maturity changed. A better Brier score can combine several statistical effects and should not be described as an isolated calibration test.

Preserve each evaluation population's definition, observation cutoff, missing-label count and selection procedure. Compare model versions on compatible populations before attributing a metric difference to an estimator change. When the decision changes the outcome or who receives a label, observational evaluation may need an experimentation or causal design rather than only a different split.

The example remains a bounded synthetic investigation. It does not establish transport to a new organization, a stable future prevalence or an acceptable operational cost. Those questions require domain evidence and explicit acceptance criteria.

Answered interview discussion#

Why can a model score perfectly yet fail on new customers? A held-out row can belong to an entity already represented in training. The model may exploit entity-specific information unavailable for new customers. Match the evaluation unit to the deployment target.

Can calibration improve without improving AUC? Yes. A strictly increasing calibration transformation preserves ordering while changing probability values. This fixture verifies identical AUC alongside improved probability scores.

Can the final test population be used to fit a calibrator? That makes it calibration training data. Preserve another independent population for the final evaluation claim.

Why is a pending label not a negative label? Pending describes observation state. Negative describes an outcome. Conflating them changes both the target and the population, particularly when delays depend on the eventual outcome.
