## Counts require an opportunity scale

Ten events in one hour and ten events in ten hours imply different rates. A count model must distinguish the number observed from the exposure during which events could occur. For independent Poisson counts,

\[
Y_i\mid e_i,x_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu_i),\qquad
\mu_i=e_i\exp(x_i^\top\beta),\qquad e_i>0.
\]

Here exposure has known units and \(\exp(x_i^\top\beta)\) is a rate per exposure unit. In a log-mean expression, \(\log e_i\) is an offset with coefficient fixed at one. Estimating an arbitrary coefficient on exposure describes a different model. It may be appropriate for a different scientific relationship, but it is not the standard constant-rate exposure correction.

The complete row NLL is

\[
\ell_i=\mu_i-y_i\log\mu_i+\log\Gamma(y_i+1).
\]

The factorial term is constant with respect to model parameters but matters for normalized probability scores and comparisons involving different observations. For an intercept-only log rate \(\eta\), \(\mu_i=e_i e^\eta\) and the score is \(\sum_i(\mu_i-y_i)\). When total count is positive, its zero gives \(\widehat\lambda=\sum_i y_i/\sum_i e_i\).

## Worked case 1: fit a common rate

Use counts \((0,2,1,4)\) observed over exposures \((1,2,1,4)\). Total count is seven and total exposure is eight, yielding rate 0.875. The code computes this analytic estimate, evaluates the complete NLL and verifies its equality with Torch's Poisson distribution. It checks that the derivative with respect to log rate is zero at this estimate.

The ordinary average count is 1.75, which is not a rate per exposure unit because the exposure varies. The average of individual rates also gives a different result and has a different weighting structure. Summing counts and exposure is justified here by the common-rate Poisson model. If rates vary systematically with conditions, an intercept-only model is too coarse even though its arithmetic is correct.

## Worked case 2: forecast a count at new exposure

For future exposure values one, two and five, the fitted model predicts expected counts 0.875, 1.75 and 4.375. The rate remains constant while the expected count scales with opportunity. A noninteger expectation is valid even though each observed Poisson count is an integer. Predictive uncertainty includes count variation, and if the rate was estimated it should additionally reflect rate uncertainty.

This distinction prevents a common reporting mistake: transferring a daily count forecast directly to a week without scaling exposure, or treating a change in observed counts caused by a longer observation window as a change in risk. The program labels its quantity `rate_per_exposure_unit`, making the unit contract visible in output rather than burying it in a comment.

## Boundary and support failures

All-zero counts have an MLE rate at zero, corresponding to log rate tending to negative infinity. The demonstration rejects this case in its finite interior fit helper. It does not conclude that the count data are invalid. A full analysis could report a boundary estimate and an appropriate one-sided uncertainty bound or introduce a declared prior.

Zero exposure with a positive count is incompatible with this model. Zero exposure with zero count is a degenerate zero-opportunity observation; handling it requires a separate explicit branch rather than taking its logarithm. The tutorial conservatively requires strictly positive exposure. Negative, fractional or nonfinite counts are rejected. Rounded continuous measurements should not be called Poisson counts merely because they appear as integers.

The Poisson conditional variance equals the conditional mean. Overdispersion can arise from heterogeneity, clustering or a different count process. A negative-binomial model or a latent-rate model may be appropriate, but changing distributions does not automatically solve dependence or selection bias. Residual and predictive checks should be conditioned on exposure and relevant predictors.

## Answered review questions

**Why is the exposure coefficient fixed?** The model assumes expected events scale linearly with opportunity when the rate is held fixed. Taking logs converts this multiplicative relationship into an additive offset.

**Can the rate estimate be distributed exactly?** In this intercept-only model, independent partitions can return count sums and exposure sums. Their global ratio equals the full-data estimate. More complex models still have additive likelihoods but usually require iterative gradient evaluations.

**What uncertainty does the printed expected count include?** It is a point prediction only. A future count distribution and a confidence interval for the underlying rate answer different questions. This chapter verifies density, units and score arithmetic; later chapters develop uncertainty explicitly.
