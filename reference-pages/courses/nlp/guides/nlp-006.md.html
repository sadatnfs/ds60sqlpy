nlp-006: N-gram models, smoothing and perplexity#

Level and prerequisites#

Beginner; about 120 minutes plus discussion. Direct prerequisites: nlp-005. Read the preceding guide if its notation or information boundaries are unfamiliar.

Purpose and mental model#

A language model assigns probabilities to sequences of language units. An n-gram model approximates the next-token distribution using a limited history. A bigram model uses only the immediately preceding token, making its assumptions and counts easy to inspect. It is a useful baseline and a foundation for understanding the next-token objective used by much larger models.

This lesson trains on three authored sentences: we like tea, we like coffee, and they like tea. The vocabulary contains five observed words plus an end-of-sequence token and an unknown token, for seven possible predictions. A beginning-of-sequence marker provides context but is never predicted. These choices determine the probability denominator and the perplexity calculation, so they are part of the model rather than formatting details.

The implementation learns real conditional count distributions with additive smoothing. It does not contain neural embeddings or attention. A finite probability for an unseen sentence means the smoothing policy assigns it mass, not that the model understands or verifies its meaning.

From sequence probability to conditional counts#

The chain rule writes p(x_1,...,x_T,EOS) as the product of conditional probabilities given the full preceding history. The bigram approximation replaces that history with only the last token. Each sentence begins at BOS and ends with a predicted EOS event. Counts reset at sentence boundaries so the end of one document does not become context for the next.

Without smoothing, maximum-likelihood probability is C(context,word)/C(context). An unseen continuation receives zero probability, which makes an entire sequence probability zero. Additive smoothing with α>0\alpha>0α>0 uses (C(context,word)+alpha)/(C(context)+alpha*V). It allocates some probability to every vocabulary item. Larger alpha moves a row closer to uniform, while very small alpha stays near empirical counts.

The unknown token provides a fixed destination for unseen evaluation words. It is included during training even though this tiny corpus has no observed unknown events. A more realistic pipeline may replace rare training words with UNK before counting. That policy changes its learned probability and should be selected without inspecting the final evaluation corpus.

Derive all four factors#

BOS has three outgoing observations: we twice and they once. With alpha one and V seven, Pr⁡(we∣BOS)=2+13+7=0.3\Pr(\mathrm{we}\mid \mathrm{BOS})=\frac{2+1}{3+7}=0.3Pr(we∣BOS)=3+72+1​=0.3. Context we appears twice, always followed by like, so P(like|we)=3/9=1/3. Context like has three continuations, tea twice and coffee once, giving P(tea|like)=3/10=0.3. Tea ends a sentence twice, so Pr⁡(EOS∣tea)=39=13\Pr(\mathrm{EOS}\mid \mathrm{tea})=\frac{3}{9}=\frac{1}{3}Pr(EOS∣tea)=93​=31​.

Multiplying gives 0.3(1/3)0.3*(1/3)=0.01. There are four predicted events, including EOS. Negative average log likelihood is −log⁡(0.01)4\frac{-\log(0.01)}{4}4−log(0.01)​, and perplexity is exp of that quantity, 10\sqrt{10}10​, approximately 3.1623. Excluding EOS from the denominator while including its probability would compute a different and inconsistent statistic.

Perplexity is measured per modeled token event. It cannot be compared casually across different tokenizers, vocabularies, boundary rules or corpora. A low value can reflect duplicated or easy evaluation text rather than useful language understanding.

Syntax anatomy, stability and diagnostic limits#

Counter stores next-token counts for one context, and the model keeps a dictionary from contexts to those counters. probability uses get rather than mutating defaultdict lookup during evaluation, so scoring cannot silently add contexts or vocabulary items. The reference snapshots the model before an unknown-word probe and checks that it remains unchanged afterward.

Log probabilities turn the product into a sum, avoiding underflow on longer sequences. Exponentiating the negative average log value produces perplexity only after the correct event count is known. Empty raw input still predicts EOS from BOS under this model, so it contains one prediction event and has perplexity ten. This differs from an API that defines an empty sequence as no scored events; either convention must be explicit.

A bigram can capture local preferences but loses longer context. We do not assert that a low perplexity sentence is factual, grammatical or useful. Evaluate a changed domain, repeated templates and unknown-token rate separately. If every unfamiliar word maps to one UNK, a model can hide important vocabulary distinctions behind a finite but uninformative score.

Worked examples#

Worked example 1: Follow the training rows#

Count we->like twice, they->like once, like->tea twice, like->coffee once, tea->EOS twice and coffee->EOS once. BOS contributes three sentence starts. The seven prediction targets are coffee, like, tea, they, we, EOS and UNK in a recorded order; BOS is excluded.

Worked example 2: Compute joint likelihood and perplexity#

For we like tea, the four smoothed factors are [0.3,13,0.3,13][0.3,\frac{1}{3},0.3,\frac{1}{3}][0.3,31​,0.3,31​]. Their product is 0.01 and perplexity is 10\sqrt{10}10​. A hand calculation provides an independent oracle for the program's sum of logs.

Worked example 3: Score an unknown without learning from it#

The evaluation sentence we like cocoa maps cocoa to UNK. Its score remains finite because alpha is positive. The vocabulary and count dictionary must be identical before and after scoring. Finite probability is smoothing behavior, not evidence that cocoa was represented with a learned semantic meaning.

import math
factors=[3/10,3/9,3/10,3/9]
joint=math.prod(factors)
perplexity=math.exp(-sum(math.log(p) for p in factors)/4)
print(joint,perplexity)
assert math.isclose(joint,.01) and math.isclose(perplexity,math.sqrt(10))

Interview reasoning#

Interview: Why smooth an n-gram model? Unseen continuations otherwise receive zero probability and make complete sequences impossible. Smoothing distributes probability beyond observed pairs under an explicit bias assumption; it does not create semantic knowledge.

Interview: Why is BOS excluded from V here? It is a conditioning marker rather than a predicted event. EOS is predicted and belongs in the denominator. Different conventions are possible, but the probability and evaluation definitions must agree.

Interview: Does lower perplexity mean better factual answers? It means better average predictive probability on the specified token events. Factuality, grounding and task utility require separate evaluation.
