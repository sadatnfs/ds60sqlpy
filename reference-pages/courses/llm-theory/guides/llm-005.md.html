Purpose and mental model#

Turn probability assignments into an interpretable loss#

A prediction system should pay a larger penalty for assigning very little probability to the event that occurs. Negative log probability does this smoothly: an observed target with probability 1 has loss zero, probability 12\frac{1}{2} has loss log(2)\log(2), and probability 14\frac{1}{4} has loss log(4)\log(4). Halving the probability adds log(2)\log(2) to the loss. This additive structure connects directly to the sequence likelihood from the previous chapter, where products of conditional probabilities became sums of losses.

For one observation with target token y, let p_y be the model's probability of that token. Its loss is −log(py)-\log(p_{y}). This can also be written as cross entropy between the model distribution p and a one-hot target distribution q: −∑iqilogpi-\sum_iq_i\log p_i. Every q entry is zero except the target's entry, which is one, so only −log(py)-\log(p_{y}) remains. The notation is useful because it makes the two roles explicit: q specifies what is being scored, while p is the model's prediction. The model should not choose its own reference target after seeing its answer.

In Example A, the two target probabilities are 0.5 and 0.25. Their losses are log(2)\log(2) and 2·log(2)2\cdot \log(2), so the total is 3·log(2)3\cdot \log(2), about 2.079442. Dividing by the two scored targets gives 1.5·log(2)1.5\cdot \log(2), about 1.039721. Exponentiating that mean gives perplexity 232{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}, about 2.828427. The total, mean and perplexity describe the same observations under different transformations; none is interchangeable with the number of correct top-ranked predictions.

One useful interpretation of perplexity comes from a uniform prediction over K choices. Each observed choice then receives probability 1K\frac{1}{K}, so its loss is log(K)\log(K) and perplexity is K. For a nonuniform model, perplexity is the exponential of its average log penalty, not a literal count of equally plausible alternatives on every row. Here it is also the reciprocal geometric mean of the two assigned probabilities: 10.5·0.25\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.5\cdot 0.25}}. Using the arithmetic mean probability would produce a different statistic.

The denominator is part of the experiment. An ignored padding slot is not a third observation with a conveniently perfect prediction. Dividing the same total by three instead of two produces log(2)\log(2), and its perplexity falls to 2. The model did not change; only an invalid denominator made the report look better. The same issue appears when comparing documents of different lengths, averaging batches of different sizes, or training only on answer tokens. Always retain the active count alongside a reduced loss.

Cross entropy is useful because it evaluates the whole probability assigned to the observed event and supplies a differentiable training objective. A model can improve it without changing its argmax, by becoming better calibrated on those targets. Conversely, a system can improve a particular task's decision accuracy without winning every likelihood comparison. Keep tokenizer, target definition, conditioning context, mask and reduction fixed when interpreting a difference. Token-level perplexity is especially sensitive to the token unit, which motivates the next two chapters.

Read cross entropy as a weighted expectation#

For active target probabilities p_1 through p_N, the empirical mean cross entropy is H=−1N∑ilogpiH=-\frac1N\sum_i\log p_i. Perplexity is exp(H)\exp(H), so algebra gives PPL=(∏i1pi)1/N\mathrm{PPL}=\left(\prod_i\frac1{p_i}\right)^{1/N}. It is the inverse geometric mean of the observed target probabilities. It is not the arithmetic mean of their reciprocals. For [.5,.25], the inverse probabilities are 2 and 4, whose geometric mean is sqrt8≈2.828; their arithmetic mean is 3. The latter is a different statistic.

A helpful special case is a uniform predictor over K candidates. Every observed target has probability1/K, giving H=log K and PPL=K\mathrm{PPL}=K. This motivates the rough interpretation of perplexity as an effective number of equally plausible choices. Real distributions are not uniform and their candidate sets may be constrained differently at each position, so this interpretation must not be turned into a literal count of alternatives considered by the model.

Cross entropy includes the uncertainty present in the data as well as mismatch between predictor and target distribution. For a fixed discrete true distribution q and model p on compatible support, H(q,p)=H(q)+KL(q||p). The entropy H(q) is not something a model can reduce by guessing better; the nonnegative KL term describes mismatch. In the lesson we observe a finite set of target events rather than know q exactly, so the empirical loss is an estimate under its stated sampling/weighting policy. This distinction matters when one dataset is intrinsically less predictable than another.

Write masking into both numerator and denominator: H=−∑imilogpi∑imiH=-\frac{\sum_i m_i\log p_i}{\sum_i m_i}, with m_i in{0,1}. An ignored event contributes neither a term nor a count. In implementation, selecting active targets before evaluating problematic logs avoids expressions such as0*infinity producing NaN. An all-ignored batch has no empirical mean. The correct response is a defined no-target policy or explicit refusal, not a fabricated zero loss that looks like perfect prediction.

Unequal document lengths expose a second denominator choice. Document A has one target of probability 0.5; document B has three targets of probability.25. The combined sum is 7log2 over four targets, so token-weighted mean is 7log2/4 and perplexity is 274{2}^{\frac{7}{4}}, about 3.364. Equal-document averaging first forms means log 2 and 2log2, then averages to 1.5log2 and perplexity sqrt8. Averaging document perplexities directly gives(2+4)/2=3, which agrees with neither policy. Aggregate losses and counts under the intended unit before exponentiating.

A low mean can hide a catastrophic rare failure. Compare target probabilities [.99,.99,.01] with [.5,.5,.5]. The first predictor is very confident and correct on two observations but assigns only.01 to the third. Its loss contributions are about.01005,.01005,4.60517, so the mean is about 1.54176 and perplexity about 4.673. The uniform two-choice predictor has mean log 2 and perplexity2. The log score strongly penalizes assigning very little mass to an event that actually occurs. An accuracy metric using only top choices would tell a different part of the story.

Tokenization and inclusion rules must remain compatible. If one model uses a single token for a string while another uses three, equal string probability can coexist with different per-token loss: probability1/8 gives NLL3log2, yielding PPL8 for one target or PPL2 for three targets. A byte-normalized score can provide a common unit only after defining which bytes, boundaries and conditional events correspond to the exact same evaluated strings. It is not enough to divide arbitrary losses by a convenient byte count while changing prompt or EOS treatment.

Interview reasoning: "Can a new model have lower perplexity but worse answers?" Yes. Perplexity measures assigned target-token probabilities under a specific representation and dataset. Answer correctness, citation support, refusal behavior and task utility have different acceptance rules. Use perplexity to assess the defined prediction objective, then evaluate the behaviors required by the application separately.

Vocabulary and concepts#

Cross entropy here: the empirical average negative log probability of the supplied target tokens under a declared weighting.

Perplexity: exp(mean negative log likelihood)\exp(\text{mean negative log likelihood}) when natural logarithms are used.

Active target: a target included in both the loss sum and denominator after an explicit mask.

Token weighting: equal contribution per active target position, not necessarily per document.

Comparable score: a measure whose target representation, inclusion rule and evaluation unit are compatible across the systems being compared.

Syntax or reasoning anatomy#

Add the negative logs for active targets, divide by their count, then exponentiate if perplexity is useful. Mask by selection before counting. A zero-probability active target has infinite loss under the declared distribution; an ignored target contributes neither a loss nor a denominator term. An all-ignored row has no defined empirical mean and should fail explicitly.

The helper exposes total_nll, active_targets, mean_nll and perplexity as separate fields. This makes a denominator error observable without inspecting printed prose. Equal-document weighting first computes each document mean and then averages documents, so a long document no longer contributes proportionally more targets. Choose the aggregation unit before comparing outcomes, and retain that choice with saved predictions.

Worked example / walkthrough#

Run Examples A and B in order in one scratch file, then run that complete file in a fresh process. Example B may reuse the imports, helpers and fixture state established in Example A. Do not treat an isolated later block as a standalone program.

Example A: Two target probabilities#

Use the same conditional probabilities for both the loss and perplexity calculation.

import math

probabilities = [0.5, 0.25]
total = -sum(math.log(p) for p in probabilities)
mean = total / 2
perplexity = math.exp(mean)
assert math.isclose(mean, 1.5 * math.log(2), rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-12)
assert math.isclose(perplexity, 2**1.5, rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-12)
print(total, mean, perplexity)

Expected: The three values are related but not interchangeable. Perplexity counts uncertainty in this particular token unit, not factual accuracy.

Example B: Ignored padding cannot improve a mean#

Append an ignored position and compare its correct denominator with a broken zero-loss average.

active_losses = [-math.log(0.5), -math.log(0.25)]
correct = sum(active_losses) / 2
wrong = sum(active_losses) / 3
assert math.isclose(correct, 1.5 * math.log(2), rel_tol=0, abs_tol=1e-12)
assert wrong < correct
print(correct, wrong)

Expected: The wrong mean looks better solely because a padding slot was counted as another observation. No model prediction improved.

Progressive hints#

  • Hint 1: Write the sum, active count and mean as three separate quantities.
  • Hint 2: Select active positions before counting them; zeroing an ignored loss is not enough.
  • Hint 3: For tokenizer comparisons, distinguish a complete-string probability from a per-token average.

Common pitfalls#

If padding lowers a reported mean loss, inspect the active-target count before changing weights or a learning rate. If perplexity is infinite, determine whether an active probability was zero or exponentiation exceeded the available numeric range. Keep a finite log score even when its exponential cannot be represented.

If a tokenizer changes, start a new experiment and label its target units. A lower per-token perplexity across incompatible vocabularies is not a direct quality ranking. A loss score also does not count correct answers or supported citations. Use an answer-level metric when that is the intended claim, with its own held-out question and family policy.
