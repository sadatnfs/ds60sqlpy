## Turn probability assignments into an interpretable loss

A prediction system should pay a larger penalty for assigning very little probability to the event that occurs. Negative log probability does this smoothly: an observed target with probability 1 has loss zero, probability 1/2 has loss log(2), and probability 1/4 has loss log(4). Halving the probability adds log(2) to the loss. This additive structure connects directly to the sequence likelihood from the previous chapter, where products of conditional probabilities became sums of losses.

For one observation with target token y, let p_y be the model's probability of that token. Its loss is -log(p_y). This can also be written as cross entropy between the model distribution p and a one-hot target distribution q: -sum_i q_i log(p_i). Every q entry is zero except the target's entry, which is one, so only -log(p_y) remains. The notation is useful because it makes the two roles explicit: q specifies what is being scored, while p is the model's prediction. The model should not choose its own reference target after seeing its answer.

In Example A, the two target probabilities are 0.5 and 0.25. Their losses are log(2) and 2*log(2), so the total is 3*log(2), about 2.079442. Dividing by the two scored targets gives 1.5*log(2), about 1.039721. Exponentiating that mean gives perplexity 2^(3/2), about 2.828427. The total, mean and perplexity describe the same observations under different transformations; none is interchangeable with the number of correct top-ranked predictions.

One useful interpretation of perplexity comes from a uniform prediction over K choices. Each observed choice then receives probability 1/K, so its loss is log(K) and perplexity is K. For a nonuniform model, perplexity is the exponential of its average log penalty, not a literal count of equally plausible alternatives on every row. Here it is also the reciprocal geometric mean of the two assigned probabilities: 1/sqrt(0.5*0.25). Using the arithmetic mean probability would produce a different statistic.

The denominator is part of the experiment. An ignored padding slot is not a third observation with a conveniently perfect prediction. Dividing the same total by three instead of two produces log(2), and its perplexity falls to 2. The model did not change; only an invalid denominator made the report look better. The same issue appears when comparing documents of different lengths, averaging batches of different sizes, or training only on answer tokens. Always retain the active count alongside a reduced loss.

Cross entropy is useful because it evaluates the whole probability assigned to the observed event and supplies a differentiable training objective. A model can improve it without changing its argmax, by becoming better calibrated on those targets. Conversely, a system can improve a particular task's decision accuracy without winning every likelihood comparison. Keep tokenizer, target definition, conditioning context, mask and reduction fixed when interpreting a difference. Token-level perplexity is especially sensitive to the token unit, which motivates the next two chapters.
